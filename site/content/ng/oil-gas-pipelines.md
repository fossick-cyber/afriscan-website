::::section{id="problem" eyebrow="The problem, in your words" title="On the right of way, between patrols" lead="Nigerian pipeline rights of way are long, narrow and hard to watch. What appears on them becomes somebody's problem: a compensation claim, a safety risk to the people living there, lost throughput or a dispute about who was there first."}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="“There are new structures inside the setback.”" icon="houses"}
Homes, shops, workshops and fences go up on and beside the right of way, most often where the line runs along a road or a town is growing towards it. Each is easier to discuss with the community while it is new.
:::
:::card{title="“Someone has been digging near the line.”" icon="excavation"}
Third-party interference shows at the surface first: fresh digging, spoil, footpaths and tracks that were not there before, and cleared ground. Patrols on a cycle often find it late.
:::
:::card{title="“We can't get to that stretch safely.”" icon="shield"}
Swamp, creek and mangrove crossings, remote savannah and insecure areas are slow, costly or unsafe to reach. Satellite screening needs no one on site, so the whole corridor is covered before a team is sent.
:::
:::card{title="“We've just taken over this asset.”" icon="building"}
Onshore and swamp assets have been changing hands. A new operator needs a dated picture of what stood around its lines, flow stations and well pads on the day it took over.
:::
:::

Pipeline vandalism and third-party interference are why most right-of-way programmes exist. NNPC's monthly report tracks "Upstream Pipeline Availability" as a headline measure and lists "pipeline incidents" among the causes of lost crude output in July 2026 ([NNPC, July 2026](https://fde-nnpc-web-cms-prod-dwfrd0hraahrbhhg.a02.azurefd.net/uploads/NNPC_Ltd_Monthly_Report_for_July_2026_6f46510d5a.pdf)). Imagery cannot see a tap or a leak. What it can show, reviewed and dated, is where the ground around the line has changed, which tells your teams where to go first.

| Instrument | What it sets for pipelines |
|---|---|
| [Oil Pipelines Act](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/view2.php?sn=425) (1956 No. 31), ss.4–6 | A permit to survey a route (ss.4–5), and 14 days' notice before entering buildings or enclosed gardens, with compensation for damage (s.6) |
| Oil Pipelines Act, s.12 | On the licence holder's application, an order ancillary to the licence can prohibit or restrict buildings, cultivation and industrial, mining or oil-mining activity "within a specified distance, not exceeding 100 feet, from the boundaries of the land" in the licence. The Act is not among the laws the Petroleum Industry Act repeals |
| [Petroleum Industry Act 2021](https://placng.org/i/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Petroleum-Industry-Act-2021.pdf), ss.90, 127, 176 | Rights of way for upstream pipelines (s.90), for gas pipelines in the midstream and downstream (s.127), and for petroleum liquids pipelines (s.176); gas and liquids transportation pipeline licences (ss.135 and 190); compensation for land acquired (s.115) |
| [Land Use Act](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/view2.php?sn=228) (1978 No. 6), ss.28–29 | Revocation of rights of occupancy for "overriding public interest", which includes land for "mining purposes or oil pipelines" (s.28); compensation for buildings at replacement cost less depreciation (s.29(4)), and community compensation paid to the community, its chief or a fund (s.29(3)) |

We report structures within **50 m and 100 m** of the centreline by default. Send the licence strip as a polygon and we add the structures inside it and within the distance of any s.12 order from its boundary (up to 100 feet, 30.48 m), or any width your company standard, lender or ESIA sets, up to six in one survey. The regulators are the Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission (NUPRC) for upstream lines and the Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority (NMDPRA) for midstream and downstream pipeline licences.

::::section{id="rights-of-way" eyebrow="What we map along the line" title="A register of what stands on the right of way, then what changed"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
We buffer the route you supply in its UTM zone (31N, 32N or 33N across Nigeria), list each structure the review confirms with its distance to the line and its band, and cut the route into 500 m stretches. Each stretch is rated for **encroachment density**: high where more than five structures stand inside the widest band, medium for one to five, low for none. It is a count rule that tells your right-of-way team where to go first, not a safety or integrity rating.

After the baseline, the route is re-surveyed on a schedule you agree with us. New and removed structures between dated surveys are flagged automatically and confirmed by a reviewer, with before-and-after views, and your team receives a notice after each survey saying what changed and where. Fresh digging, spoil, trenches and new tracks on or near the line are flagged the same way, so patrols know where to look. They do not replace patrols.

In the dry season, bush burning can run up to above-ground installations such as manifolds, valve stations and pig traps. We send notices of satellite-detected fire hotspots near them and map the burnt area afterwards.

::::section{id="corridors" tone="alt" eyebrow="Where the lines run" title="From the creeks of the Delta to the gas lines of the north"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Crude trunklines such as the Trans-Niger and Trans-Forcados lines cross the swamps, creeks and farmland of the Niger Delta to the export terminals. Gas networks such as the Escravos–Lagos Pipeline System run west towards Lagos, and new gas trunklines such as the Ajaokuta–Kaduna–Kano (AKK) line carry gas north through savannah and growing towns. Each of those settings changes differently: fishing settlements and creek landings in the swamp, farm clearings and new roads on dry land, and fast-growing towns where lines meet the road network.

The survey is set up for the stretch, not for the country. Dense mangrove and tree canopy hide small structures, so swamp stretches lean on reviewer checks, with radar comparisons for larger clearing; small structures under canopy can be missed; urban stretches need the finest imagery and the most review; savannah stretches are where new tracks and clearings show best.
::::
::::col
### Planning around the rainy season {#rainy-season}

Optical satellite imagery needs clear skies, and cloud cover in the south is heavy through the long rainy season. We plan new captures for clearer spells, start from the best recent archive scenes, and say in each report which dates the imagery comes from.

Radar satellite comparisons show larger changes, such as clearing, earthworks and new large buildings, even under rainy-season cloud. They do not show individual huts, so radar flags are followed up with optical imagery or a drone check once the weather allows.

::::section{id="niger-delta" eyebrow="Niger Delta lines and facilities" title="Visible signs, flagged for checking" lead="Sites showing visible signs of artisanal refining, and oil-stained or dead vegetation near your pipelines and facilities, mapped and flagged for your security and environment teams to check."}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
Around some Delta lines and facilities the question is not only what has been built, but what the ground shows. From dated imagery, reviewers look for the surface signs that go with artisanal refining sites and oil-impacted ground: clearings with dark-stained soil, pits and small ponds, stained or dead vegetation along a creek or beside the right of way, burn scars, and fresh tracks or landings that lead to them. Satellite-detected fire hotspots near your lines add a second signal.

Each site is flagged with its location, its distance to your line, the imagery date and the reviewer's note on what is visible, so your environment, security and field teams can decide where to check first. Repeat surveys show which sites are new, which have grown and which have gone.

::::section{id="handover" eyebrow="Fields, flow stations and handovers" title="A dated baseline when an asset changes hands"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Onshore and swamp assets have been passing from international operators to Nigerian companies. A dated handover record, made from archived imagery and a new survey, shows what stood around the lines, flow stations, manifolds and well pads on the day of transfer: the structures and their distances, the tracks, the cleared and disturbed ground, and roughly when each appeared. It supports the new owner in later compensation and community discussions; it does not decide them.

After the handover, perimeter surveys show new structures, cleared ground and new tracks at the edges of flow stations and well pads between dates. Fences and gates are checked by a reviewer on drone imagery, because they are too thin to detect on satellite imagery.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="Your own facilities are not encroachment"}
An operator's flow stations, manifolds, well pads, camps and yards are part of the asset. We record them as the operator's installations, never as encroachment, and keep them out of the encroachment register unless you ask for them to be listed separately.

::::section{id="new-gas-lines" tone="alt" eyebrow="New gas lines" title="Route options and a baseline before settlement follows"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
The cheapest structure to deal with is the one the route avoids. We count the structures along each alternative alignment within the same bands, so route engineers and the land team can compare options on the land they would affect, alongside their engineering and environmental criteria. Slope, drainage crossings and ground that has flooded in past satellite records are screened along each option.

Once the route is fixed, a dated baseline records the corridor before the new access road brings building, and estimated households help plan consultation and compensation enumeration. The comparison is one input to route selection, not a full alternatives assessment.
::::
::::col
### For compensation and resettlement {#compensation}

A dated structure register with imagery, coordinates and IDs supports the compensation enumeration for a right of way under the Land Use Act and, on lender-financed projects, the census and asset inventory behind an IFC Performance Standard 5 cut-off date. It does not replace either: field teams still enumerate people and assets, and categories describe what the imagery shows. See [ESIA and resettlement baselines in Nigeria](key:project-finance-esia).

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Drone checks near pipelines" title="Where a drone helps, and what it needs first"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Satellite screening narrows the search; a drone survey of the flagged stretches then captures the detail your team needs, subject to the NCAA and ONSA authorisations each job requires. In Nigeria that means an operator with an RPAS Operator Certificate whose Operations Specifications cover the job, registered drones, ONSA security clearance and End-User Certificates for imported equipment.

Pipeline stretches lined with homes are the hard case: Part 21 keeps flights away from populated areas and at least 30 m from people not involved in the operation unless specifically approved. Around Port Harcourt and the terminals, NCAA warns of low-flying helicopters and requires ATC clearance in controlled airspace. Afridrone is working towards the authorisations Nigeria requires, and every drone proposal lists the approvals its flights need.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="legal" title="Near flow stations and terminals"}
Part 21 sets no numeric exclusion zone around oil and gas installations. The owner's written consent, NUPRC's rule that drone-based security services show NCAA and ONSA approvals, and the Official Secrets Act around military sites apply instead. We plan any flight near a facility through your security department and mask sensitive sites in every deliverable.

::::section{id="local-content" tone="alt" eyebrow="Nigerian content" title="What the NOGICD Act means for this service"}
Oil and gas contracts in Nigeria are awarded under the Nigerian Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act 2010, which gives first consideration to Nigerian companies and sets minimum Nigerian content for listed services, including photogrammetric surveying, chart and map production, survey and positioning support, and data management. Contractors to upstream operators also need an NUPRC service permit and registration on NOGIC JQS. Which schedule line a mapping or drone scope falls under is for your Nigerian content team and NCDMB to settle; the [Nigerian content and procurement notes](/ng/nigerian-content) set out the detail and what to ask any supplier for.
