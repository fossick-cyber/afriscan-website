faq:
  - q: Which widths do you measure in Nigeria?
    a: The ones that apply to your asset. For power lines we default to the NESIS right of way for the voltage (50 m for 330 kV, 30 m for 132 kV, 11 m for 33 kV and 11 kV, split equally either side of the centre line). For pipelines we measure from the centreline you supply, from the boundary of the licence strip if you send it as a polygon, and to the distance in any Oil Pipelines Act s.12 order. Up to six widths can be reported in one survey.
  - q: Do you need anyone on site to start?
    a: No. A survey starts from your route file and dated satellite imagery, open building datasets or imagery you already hold, so no one travels and no drone flies. That matters on swamp and creek stretches and where access is unsafe. Drone detail is added only where a stretch needs it, subject to the NCAA and ONSA authorisations each job requires.
  - q: Does this replace pipeline patrols or security contracts?
    a: No. It shows your patrol, right-of-way and asset-protection teams where the ground has changed, so they go there first. AfriScan is a remote-sensing and mapping service, not a security service.
  - q: Can you detect taps, leaks or oil theft?
    a: No. Imagery cannot see a tap, a leak or the condition of the pipe. We map what is visible at the surface, such as new structures, fresh digging, spoil, new tracks, cleared ground and visible signs of artisanal refining, and flag it for your teams to check.
  - q: Who sees our route and the results?
    a: Only the contacts you name. We never publish maps of your assets or of the settlements around them without your written permission, military and other sensitive sites are masked, and the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 applies to any personal data in the imagery.
  - q: Can the register be shared with host communities or regulators?
    a: That is your decision. The register records what stood where, and on which imagery date, so it can support community engagement, compensation enumeration, regulatory reporting and legal processes. Whether a structure is authorised is for you and the authorities to decide, not for us.

**The law behind the register** Oil Pipelines Act s.12 · Petroleum Industry Act 2021 ss.90, 127, 176 · Land Use Act ss.28–29 · NESIS Regulations 2015 s.3.1 · EIA Act 1992 · Nig.CARs 2023 Part 21 · NDPA 2023 · IFC Performance Standard 5

::::section{id="law" eyebrow="Already in the law" title="The setbacks your register is measured against" lead="Nigerian law draws lines along pipelines and power lines. A setback only protects the asset if someone can see what stands inside it, and when it arrived."}
:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="Pipelines: up to 100 feet beyond the licence strip" icon="pipeline" eyebrow="Oil Pipelines Act, s.12"}
On the licence holder's application, the President can attach an order to a pipeline licence prohibiting or restricting buildings, cultivation and industrial or mining activity "within a specified distance, not exceeding 100 feet, from the boundaries of the land" in the licence ([Oil Pipelines Act](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/view2.php?sn=425)). We measure to that distance from the strip you send us.
:::
:::card{title="Power lines: no structures under the line" icon="power" eyebrow="NESIS Regulations 2015"}
NERC's standard sets rights of way of 50 m for 330 kV, 30 m for 132 kV and 11 m for 33 kV and 11 kV lines, divided equally either side of the centre line, and says "no structures shall be built under the Overhead line Right of Way" ([NESIS, s.3.1](https://nemsa.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/NESIS-Regulations-2015-1-1.pdf)).
:::
:::card{title="Compensation: buildings counted at a date" icon="calendar" eyebrow="Land Use Act, ss.28–29"}
Land can be revoked for "mining purposes or oil pipelines" (s.28), with compensation for buildings at replacement cost less depreciation (s.29(4)) ([Land Use Act](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/view2.php?sn=228)). A dated record of which structures stood where is what compensation enumeration and an IFC Performance Standard 5 cut-off date are built on.
:::
:::

::::section{id="problem" tone="alt" eyebrow="Why it matters" title="What changes between patrols" lead="Nigerian trunklines and transmission lines cross swamp, creeks, farmland, savannah and fast-growing towns. The ground beside them changes faster than any patrol cycle can record."}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="“There are new buildings on the right of way.”" icon="houses"}
Homes, shops, workshops and fences go up inside setbacks, most often where a line runs beside a road or a town grows towards it. Each one is easier to discuss while it is new and few.
:::
:::card{title="“Someone has been digging near the line.”" icon="excavation"}
Third-party interference shows at the surface as fresh digging, spoil, new footpaths and tracks, and cleared ground. We flag those between surveys so field teams know where to look first.
:::
:::card{title="“Settlement is following the new gas line.”" icon="route"}
New trunklines bring access roads, and access roads bring building. A dated baseline taken when the line is laid is the record every later compensation or encroachment discussion goes back to.
:::
:::card{title="“We can't get to that stretch safely.”" icon="shield"}
Swamp and creek crossings, remote savannah and insecure areas are slow, costly or unsafe to reach. Satellite screening needs no one on site, so the whole corridor is covered before anyone decides where to send a team.
:::
:::

The operators' own reports show why the record matters. NNPC's monthly report for July 2026 tracks "Upstream Pipeline Availability" as a headline measure and lists "pipeline incidents" among the causes of lost crude output ([NNPC, July 2026](https://fde-nnpc-web-cms-prod-dwfrd0hraahrbhhg.a02.azurefd.net/uploads/NNPC_Ltd_Monthly_Report_for_July_2026_6f46510d5a.pdf)). In June 2026 TCN reported damage to six towers on its Apir–Lafia 330 kV lines ([TCN, 2 June 2026](https://www.tcn.org.ng/blog_post_sidebar262.php)). Imagery cannot prevent either; it can show where the ground around the asset has changed, and when.

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Satellite first, drones where the permits allow" title="Two routes to the picture, and the rules for each"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
**Satellite and your own imagery.** A survey starts from your route file and dated satellite imagery, open building datasets or the orthophotos your own drone teams already fly. No one travels and no drone flies, however long the line. We found no rule that requires a licence to buy or analyse commercial satellite imagery in Nigeria; counsel should confirm it for your project.

**Drone detail.** Where a stretch needs more detail than satellite imagery shows, a drone survey captures it, subject to the NCAA and ONSA authorisations each job requires. In Nigeria that means an RPAS Operator Certificate, registered drones, ONSA security clearance, End-User Certificates for imported equipment and ATC clearance in controlled airspace, and flights over populated stretches or along high-tension lines need specific approval. Afridrone is working towards the authorisations Nigeria requires, and every drone proposal lists the approvals its flights need and when each must be in place.
