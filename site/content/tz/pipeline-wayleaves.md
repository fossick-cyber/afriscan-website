  - q: Does this replace our patrols and line monitoring?
    a: No. Pipeline operators run their own patrols, control-room monitoring and community programmes, and some lay fibre-optic cable along the line for monitoring. The register adds something those systems are not built to give, a dated, reviewed record of the structures, cleared ground, tracks and excavations in and around the right of way, so patrol, land and community teams know where the ground has changed. Patrols, response and security stay with your own teams.
  - q: Can you detect leaks, illegal taps or theft?
    a: No. Imagery cannot see a tap, a leak or the condition of the pipe. We flag what is visible at the surface, such as fresh digging, spoil, new tracks and footpaths across the line, cleared ground and new structures, so your field teams know where to check.
  - q: Which widths do you measure against?
    a: The wayleave width in your own acquisition or agreement, and the bands you choose inside and around it, for example 0–15 m, 15–30 m and 30–60 m from the centreline, up to six in one survey. We found no single statutory width for a pipeline wayleave in Tanzanian law, so the widths come from you. If you send the wayleave as a polygon, we report what stands inside it as well.
  - q: Can the register support our lenders' environmental and social reporting?

On a pipeline financed under the IFC Performance Standards or the Equator Principles, the register records the structures in and around the right of way on each survey date, what is new since the cut-off date, and where a grievance point sits against the imagery history. It supports your census, resettlement and livelihood monitoring; it does not replace them or decide eligibility.

::::section{id="problem" eyebrow="The problem, in your words" title="Inside the wayleave, between patrols" lead="A pipeline wayleave in Tanzania can cross hundreds of kilometres of village land, farmland and bush, and pass the edge of towns that did not exist when it was laid. What appears on it becomes somebody's problem: a safety hazard, a future compensation cost, or a dispute about who was there first."}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="“There are new structures inside the wayleave.”" icon="houses"}
Houses, shops, workshops and fences can go up on and beside the right of way, especially where a line passes a trading centre or runs near a road. A structure is easier to discuss with the village council and the owner while it is new and alone.
:::
:::card{title="“Someone has been digging near the line.”" icon="excavation"}
Works near a buried line show at the surface first: fresh digging, spoil, trenches, footpaths and tracks that were not there before. Altering, removing or connecting to a pipeline without the licensee's consent is an offence under the Petroleum Act, s.240, and under s.240(4) an offence of economic and organised crime.
:::
:::card{title="“The line is built. What is our baseline?”" icon="calendar"}
Once the pipe is buried and the right of way reinstated, the land team needs a dated picture of what stands in and around it on day one of operation, to measure everything after against.
:::
:::card{title="“Our lenders want dated evidence.”" icon="file-check"}
Environmental and social monitoring on a lender-financed line asks what has changed around the right of way since the cut-off date, and where. Site visits are periodic; dated imagery fills the gaps between them.
:::

::::section{id="law" tone="alt" eyebrow="The law in Tanzania" title="What the register is measured against" lead="A summary of the public rules as of 27 September 2026, for orientation. It is not legal advice; the full explanation, with sources, is in our wayleave-law guide."}
| Instrument | What it sets for pipelines |
|---|---|
| [Petroleum Act, Cap. 392](https://www.ewura.go.tz/uploads/documents/en-1786364531-The%20Petroleum%20Act.pdf), s.185 | A licensee takes "all necessary measures to secure acquisition of rights of way for laying, operation and repair of gas pipelines", subject to the Gas Utilisation Master Plan and in consultation with EWURA |
| Petroleum Act, s.186 | The licensee acquires "wayleave around existing and future gas infrastructure and petroleum installations"; owners on or bordering a wayleave "shall not interfere with a licensee's acquired rights"; compensation disputes go under the Land Acquisition Act |
| Petroleum Act, s.240 | Offences for altering, removing or connecting to "any pipeline or gas installations without consent of the licensee", including attempts and assistance |
| Land Act, Cap. 113, ss.161, 166, 186–187 | A wayleave runs with the land and binds every occupier; compensation for trees, crops and buildings; the Commissioner's removal orders for obstructions and encroachments on a public right of way, and the offence of not complying |
| Tanzania-Zambia Pipeline Act, Cap. 40, s.5 | The statute behind one cross-border products pipeline; we found no strip width in it |

**Safety zones are upstream.** The Petroleum Act's s.203 safety zones, whose extent PURA determines, sit in the Part on upstream operations. They surround wells and upstream processing, not crude or products pipelines and their pump stations. For gas fields, gas transmission lines and the LNG site, see [gas pipelines and LNG in Tanzania](/tz/gas-pipelines-and-lng).

We found no single statutory width for a pipeline wayleave, so we report the bands you set from your own acquisition, agreement or company standard. [Wayleave law in Tanzania](/tz/wayleave-law#petroleum)

::::section{id="register" eyebrow="What we map along the line" title="A register of what stands in the wayleave, then what changed"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
We buffer the route you supply in its UTM zone, list each structure the review confirms with its distance to the line and its band, and cut the route into 500 m stretches. Each stretch is rated for **encroachment density**: high where more than five structures stand inside the widest band, medium for one to five, low for none. It is a count rule that tells your wayleave officers where to go first, not a safety or integrity rating.

After the baseline, the route is re-surveyed on a schedule you agree with us. New and removed structures between dated surveys are flagged automatically and confirmed by a reviewer, with before-and-after views, and your team receives a notice after each survey saying what changed and where. Fresh digging, spoil, trenches and new tracks across the line are flagged the same way, so patrols know where to look. They do not replace patrols.

Every report names the imagery and its capture date, so the register can be read against a valuation date, a cut-off date or the day the line entered operation.

::::section{id="operations" tone="alt" eyebrow="From construction to operation" title="A dated record for the life of the line"}

A cross-border crude pipeline, most of its length in Tanzania, is nearing completion and moving from construction into operation. When a line goes into service, the land questions change. The construction corridor is reinstated, and the next questions are about what is built near the pipe, where new tracks cross it and where ground is dug.

A baseline survey soon after reinstatement records the structures, tracks and cleared ground in the right of way and the bands around it, stretch by stretch. Scheduled re-surveys then show what is new, what has gone and where settlement is growing towards the line. The record sits next to the operator's own patrols, control-room monitoring and community monitoring programmes; it does not replace them.

::::section{id="lenders" eyebrow="Environmental and social compliance" title="Dated evidence for lenders' monitoring"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
Lender-financed pipelines report against the IFC Performance Standards and, for banks that have adopted them, the Equator Principles. Performance Standard 5 asks for a census and a cut-off date for eligibility that is "well documented and disseminated", and it has no duty to compensate those who encroach on the project area after a cut-off date that was clearly established and made public (paras 12 and 23). Tanzanian law points the same way: the High Court has held that developments made after the valuation and notice are not compensable.

Between site visits, a dated register answers the monitoring questions directly: how many structures stand in and around the right of way on each survey date, which are new since the cut-off date, and where resettlement sites and host villages are growing. For a single grievance point, a location check sets every dated image covering it side by side, so the grievance team can see what was there on each date.
::::
::::col

::::section{id="new-lines" tone="alt" eyebrow="New lines" title="Route options and a baseline before the valuation"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
New cross-border refined-products pipelines are being planned in Tanzania, and a products pipeline built in the 1960s now runs through towns that have grown up around it. A new line starts with route options. We count the structures along each alternative alignment within the same bands, so route engineers and the land team can compare options on the land they would affect, alongside their engineering and environmental criteria. Slope, drainage crossings and ground that has flooded in past satellite records are screened along each option.

Once the route is chosen, a dated baseline records the corridor before the valuation, and a second on the cut-off date shows what appeared in between. The comparison is one input to route selection, not a full alternatives assessment.
::::
::::col
### Village land along the route {#village-land}

Where a route crosses village land, the transfer runs under the Village Land Act, s.4: a gazetted notice at least 90 days ahead, the village council informing the villagers affected, the village assembly's approval for land under 250 ha, and no transfer until compensation is agreed or the High Court approves an interim payment (s.4(8)). A structure baseline before the notice, and another when compensation is agreed, give the valuers two fixed points. See [wayleave law in Tanzania](/tz/wayleave-law#village-land).

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Drone checks near pipelines" title="Where a drone helps, and what it needs first"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Satellite screening narrows the search; a drone survey of the flagged stretches then captures the detail your team needs, subject to the TCAA approvals and security clearances each job requires. Under the Civil Aviation (Remotely Piloted Aircraft Systems) Regulations, 2026, that means an operator with a Tanzanian principal place of business and an RPAS Operator Certificate, a security clearance, registered drones, vetted pilots and TCAA's authorisation for each operation, with a risk assessment submitted at least 14 days ahead.

A wayleave lined with homes is the hard case: flights keep 50 m from people and structures not under the operator's control unless TCAA approves otherwise. Afridrone is working towards Tanzanian drone approvals, and every drone proposal names the company that will fly and the approvals its flights need.
::::
::::col

::::section{id="local-content" tone="alt" eyebrow="Local content" title="What the Petroleum Act means for this service"}
Licence holders, contractors and subcontractors in Tanzania's petroleum sector "shall give preference to goods which are produced or available in Tanzania and services which are rendered by Tanzanian citizens or local companies" (Petroleum Act, s.219(1)). Goods and services not available locally are supplied by a company in a joint venture with a local company that owns at least 25 per cent of it, or as the regulations provide (s.219(2)–(3)). A "local company" is incorporated under the Companies Act and wholly owned by Tanzanian citizens, or is in a joint venture or partnership with Tanzanian citizens holding at least 15 per cent (s.219(9)). Each AfriScan proposal for petroleum work states which company contracts and how the proposal meets s.219; the [notes for working with us in Tanzania](/tz/working-with-afriscan) set out the rest.
