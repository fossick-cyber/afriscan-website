::::section{id="rights-of-way" tone="alt" eyebrow="Rights of way and protection zones" title="A register of the structures inside your right of way" lead="The core service: the structures the review confirms within the distances you choose, each measured to your line, and each stretch of route rated so your team knows where to go first."}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
We measure from the route you supply, in its local UTM zone, so distances are in true metres. You choose up to six bands: a statutory protection strip, your company standard, a lender's requirement, or a wider band to see pressure building before it reaches the line. Counts are cumulative, so "within 100 m" includes everything within 50 m.

The route is then cut into 500 m stretches and each is rated for **encroachment density**: high where more than five structures stand inside the widest band, medium for one to five, low for none. It is a count rule that tells you where to send people first, not a safety or integrity rating. The [methodology](/methodology#rating) sets out every rule.

After the baseline, the same route is re-surveyed on a schedule you agree with us. New and removed structures between dated surveys are flagged automatically and confirmed by a reviewer, and your team receives a notice after each survey saying what changed and where. Fresh digging, spoil heaps, trenches and earthworks on or near the right of way are flagged in the same way, so patrols know where to look. They do not replace patrols.
::::
::::col
### What you receive

:::checklist
- A structure register: ID, coordinates, distance to the line, band and chainage
- Structure counts per unit length for your class-location reviews, with main buildings and outbuildings separated
- An encroachment-density rating for each 500 m stretch
- New and removed structures between dated surveys, with before-and-after views
- A change notice after each scheduled re-survey
- Settlement growth trends and new tracks around the line
- Fresh excavation and earthworks near the line, flagged for checking
- Fire-hotspot notices near above-ground installations
- Drone checks of flagged stretches, subject to permits
:::

::::section{id="new-pipelines" tone="alt" eyebrow="New pipelines and loops" title="Weigh the land before the route is fixed"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
For a new line, a loop or a lateral, the cheapest structure to deal with is the one the route avoids. We count the structures along each alternative alignment within the same bands, so your route engineers and land team can compare options on the land they would affect, alongside their own engineering and environmental criteria.

Each option can also be screened for steep and erosion-prone stretches, drainage crossings and ground seen as water in past satellite records. Once the route is fixed, the same method gives a dated baseline of the corridor before settlement follows the new access road, and estimated households to plan consultation and the resettlement census.

The comparison is an input to route selection, not a full alternatives assessment, and the terrain work is screening, not a geotechnical or hydraulic study.
::::
::::col
### What you receive

:::checklist
- Side-by-side structure counts for each alignment, by band
- Slope, erosion and drainage-crossing screening per stretch
- Flood-exposure screening from past satellite water records
- A dated baseline register once the route is fixed
- Estimated households, with the assumptions stated
:::

::::section{id="lng-sites" eyebrow="LNG, gas-processing plants and field sites" title="Structures around plants, licence areas and resettlement villages"}
Around an LNG plant, a gas-processing facility or a compressor station the line becomes a boundary, and the question becomes a ring: what stands inside the site or licence area, what stands in a band around it, and what sits inside the safety and exclusion zones your engineers have drawn. We map all three, list each structure with its distance to the boundary or the source, and show how settlement around the plant, its access roads and its resettlement villages grows between surveys.

For large gas projects, the resettlement cut-off date is often the most expensive date in the schedule. A dated register of the structures in the footprint, with reviewer categories such as main building, outbuilding or under construction, supports your census and asset inventory under IFC Performance Standard 5. Re-surveys of resettlement villages and buffers then show what has changed since.

During construction, dated drone orthophotos compared side by side show earthworks and laydown progress, and inventories of tanks, containers, laydown areas and heavy plant record what the imagery shows on each survey date. Detection of specific objects is tuned and checked on part of your area before it runs on the rest.

::::section{id="handover" tone="alt" eyebrow="Field sites and asset handovers" title="A dated baseline when onshore assets change hands"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
When wells, flowlines and plants are sold, farmed out or handed back, the new owner inherits the land around them as well as the steel. A dated handover record fixes what stood around each well pad, flow station, flowline and plant on the day, drawn from archived imagery, a current structure register and an evidence pack, so later questions about who arrived when can be checked against the record.

At well pads and flow stations, boundary and perimeter change between dates shows new structures, cleared ground and new tracks at the edge of the site, with fence lines and gates checked by a reviewer on drone imagery.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="Your own facilities are not encroachment"}
An operator's plants, well pads, camps and yards are part of the asset. We record them as the operator's installations, never as encroachment, and they stay out of the encroachment register unless you ask us to list them separately.
:::

A handover baseline supports the new owner in later disputes. It does not decide them, and it is not a title, tenure or valuation check.

### Evidence and grievances {#evidence}

When a structure, a claim or a community concern needs a record, we package the dated imagery, the register and the maps with a manifest of file fingerprints and an independent timestamp, so you can show what was on the ground and when. The timestamp shows that the files existed at that time and have not changed since; it does not prove how the imagery was captured.

For a single point or claim, a location sheet gathers every dated image that covers it, so your grievance team can see what was there on each date. Evidence packs support legal and community processes. They do not decide claims.
::::
::::col
### After cyclones and floods {#after-events}

After a cyclone or flood, radar imagery maps the flooded area even under cloud, and a reviewer checks structures and assets along your route before and after the event on optical or drone imagery captured once the weather clears. Radar comparisons also show larger ground changes, such as clearing and earthworks, in months when cloud hides the ground from optical satellites.

Post-event work depends on imagery captured after the event and covers visible damage only. It is not loss adjustment or a structural assessment, and it is not an emergency service.

:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="Mozambique" icon="map" eyebrow="Português · English"}
The regulator for petroleum operations is the Instituto Nacional de Petróleo (INP). Resettlement follows [Decreto n.º 31/2012](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/644941485846310129/pdf/SFG2931-REVISED-RP-PORTUGUESE-P161351-Box402883B-PUBLIC-Disclosed-2-1-2017.pdf), and Lei n.º 8/2026 art. 61 puts resettlement costs on the rights holder. Reports can be delivered in Portuguese for land services and district government. Drone flights are regulated by [IACM](https://www.iacm.gov.mz/aeronaves-nao-tripulados/), and aerial surveys also need authorisation under [Lei n.º 6/2024](https://archive.gazettes.africa/archive/mz/2024/mz-government-gazette-series-i-dated-2024-06-04-no-108.pdf), including for handing the survey data to you (art. 13). Where access is restricted, satellite-first work lets a baseline start without a site visit.
:::
:::card{title="South Africa" icon="map" eyebrow="English"}
Pipelines run in servitudes, and the vocabulary is servitude, wayleave and third-party interference. NERSA issues the licences for petroleum and piped-gas pipelines under the Petroleum Pipelines Act 60 of 2003 and the Gas Act 48 of 2001. Commercial drone work needs a UASOC and an Air Service Licence under [Civil Aviation Regulations Part 101](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202303/48228rg11556gon3170.pdf), so where you already fly your own drones we analyse that imagery. If a register is to be used as evidence about unlawful conduct, POPIA's prior-authorisation rule (s57) may apply to your organisation: take advice first.
:::
:::card{title="Nigeria" icon="map" eyebrow="English"}
Upstream assets sit with NUPRC, midstream and downstream pipeline licences with NMDPRA, and Nigerian content with NCDMB. Land for pipelines can be taken under the [Land Use Act](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/view2.php?sn=228) (s.28), with compensation under s.29. Drone work needs an NCAA operator certificate under [Nig.CARs Part 21](https://ncaa.gov.ng/media/wabn2axn/nigcars-part-21-remotely-piloted-aircraft-system.pdf) with security clearance built into the process, and notice to OSGOF before aerial photography for survey, so satellite screening is the practical first step. We describe this work as remote sensing and mapping of land and assets.
