  - q: Can you tell us who is digging, or how many people are on site?
    a: No. We map the ground, not people. Pits, spoil heaps, water-filled excavations and disturbed ground are mapped, new sites are flagged between dates, and a reviewer confirms them before they reach you.
  - q: Can you see underground workings?
    a: No. Underground workings and shafts under cover are not visible from above. We map surface disturbance only.
  - q: Do you model dam breaks or blast zones?
    a: No. Your engineers supply the zones. We list the structures inside them, with each one's location and distance to the source, and show how settlement in them changes over time.
  - q: We already fly our own drones. Can you use our imagery?
    a: Yes. Send the orthophotos or scenes you already have, and we run the same structure, change and boundary analysis on them. Results depend on the imagery's quality and resolution.
  - q: Does your baseline replace our resettlement census?

  - q: Can you confirm our concession boundary or title?

  - q: How do you follow rehabilitation?

::::section{id="concessions" tone="alt" eyebrow="Concession edges and boundaries" title="What stands inside your right, and in a ring around it"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
We start from the boundary you give us: a mining right, a lease, a surface-use area or a plant perimeter. Structures are mapped inside it, in a ring around it of the width you choose, or both, with an optional setback inside the boundary so the fence line and the ground just inside it are counted separately. Each structure is listed with its distance to the boundary.

Across a whole concession, the same work produces a structure layer your GIS team can load straight into its own mapping. Between dated surveys, new structures, cleared ground and new tracks at the boundary are flagged automatically and confirmed by a reviewer, and a trend of built-up land over the years shows where settlement pressure on the edge is growing. Fence lines and gates are checked by a reviewer on drone imagery; they are too thin to detect reliably on satellite imagery.

We map what is there. Whether a structure is authorised, and what happens next, is for the rights holder and the authorities to decide.
::::
::::col
### What you receive

:::checklist
- A register of structures inside the right and in the ring, with distance to the boundary
- A structure layer across the whole concession
- Boundary-change layers between dated surveys, reviewer-confirmed
- Settlement growth trends at the edge
- New tracks and access routes into the right
- Scheduled re-surveys, with a notice of what changed

::::section{id="resettlement" eyebrow="Expansions and resettlement" title="Record the footprint before the census"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
A new pit, a waste dump or a tailings facility often means resettlement, and the cut-off date is when the record matters most. We produce a dated record of the structures in the footprint on the imagery date, with coordinates, IDs and reviewer categories such as main building, outbuilding, livestock enclosure or under construction. Where villages are dense or roofs sit under trees, reviewers mark structures by hand on high-resolution imagery rather than rely on automatic detection.

That record supports your census and asset inventory under IFC Performance Standard 5. It does not replace them. Estimated households, with the assumptions stated, help your team plan fieldwork and consultation. An evidence pack fixes the record with file fingerprints and an independent timestamp.
::::
::::col

::::section{id="tailings" tone="alt" eyebrow="Tailings, blast and hazard zones" title="A current list of the structures inside your zones"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
Your engineers define the zones: a dam-break inundation area downstream of a tailings facility, a blast exclusion radius, a flood line or a safety setback. We list the structures inside each zone, with each one's location and distance to the source, and show how settlement inside the zone changes between surveys. The list can be refreshed on a schedule agreed with you, so the emergency-preparedness plan is working from the current picture rather than the one from the last site visit.

Estimated households inside a zone help preparedness planning, always as estimates with the assumptions stated. Screening for ground seen as water in past satellite records adds context, and where roofs are hard to read, reviewers mark structures by hand.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="The zone is yours"}
We do not model dam breaks, blasts or floods, and we make no dam-safety conformance claim. We list the structures inside the zones your engineers supply, which keeps the engineering judgement where it belongs.

::::section{id="mineral-sands" eyebrow="Mineral-sands mines" title="A mine path that moves: baselines ahead of it, rehabilitation behind it" lead="A mineral-sands operation advances across its deposit, so the footprint that matters changes every year. The land ahead of the mine path needs a dated record before it is mined, and the land behind it needs evidence of rehabilitation."}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
Dredge and dry mining move through a deposit along a planned mine path, and an expansion or a move into a new ore zone brings new land into the footprint, often land with homesteads, fields and outbuildings on it. Each block ahead of the path can then need its own cut-off date and its own census.

We produce a dated register of the structures in each block ahead of the mine path, with coordinates, stable IDs and reviewer categories such as main building, outbuilding, livestock enclosure or under construction, and estimated households with the assumptions stated, so your resettlement team can plan the census and consultation block by block. The register supports your census and asset inventory under IFC Performance Standard 5; it does not replace them. After each block's cut-off date, re-surveys flag new structures, confirmed by a reviewer, and an evidence pack fixes each block's record.

Around the operation, a ring survey shows how settlement near the lease boundary, the plant and the haul roads grows between dates, and new tracks into the lease are mapped. Behind the path, a land-cover baseline recorded before mining gives the reference, and revegetation of rehabilitated land is tracked between dates from Copernicus Sentinel data and, where flown, drone surveys. It shows the trend in vegetation cover; it is not an ecological assessment or a closure sign-off.
::::
::::col
### What you receive

:::checklist
- A dated register for each block ahead of the mine path
- Reviewer categories and estimated households for each block
- Re-surveys after each block's cut-off date, with new structures confirmed
- An evidence pack for each block's record
- Structures in a ring around the lease, the plant and the haul roads
- A land-cover baseline before mining
- Revegetation of rehabilitated land behind the path

::::section{id="gemstone-concessions" tone="alt" eyebrow="Gemstone and other concessions" title="Ground disturbance across a concession, mapped for its management and safety"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
On gemstone concessions, and on other titles where artisanal digging is common, surface disturbance can spread across large areas between visits: pits, spoil heaps, water-filled excavations, cleared ground and new tracks. The rights holder needs to know where the ground is changing to manage the concession and its dealings with communities and authorities, and open and water-filled pits are a safety hazard whose locations are worth knowing.

We map pits, disturbed ground, spoil heaps and water-filled excavations inside and around the concession, flag new and growing sites between dated surveys, and list each with its location, its size on the imagery and its distance to the concession boundary or to the zones you define. New tracks into the concession are mapped between dates, and worked-out ground can be followed as it revegetates. The screening between surveys and the tuning of detection to your imagery work as described under [surface excavation](key:mining#artisanal-mining) below.

For safety planning, the zones can be the ones your team draws around roads, fields, water points or your own pits and plant, so each flagged pit is listed against the places where it matters. Where access is restricted, satellite-first work keeps the record going without a site visit.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="The ground, never the people"}
The record describes where the ground has been dug, when it changed and how large the disturbed area is. It never counts, identifies or describes people, it is not a security service, and underground workings are not visible from above. We never publish site maps, and results go only to the contacts you name.
:::

::::section{id="rehabilitation" tone="alt" eyebrow="Rehabilitation and closure" title="Dated evidence of rehabilitation, clearing and volumes"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Revegetation on rehabilitated land is followed between dates from Copernicus Sentinel data, alongside wider vegetation and land-cover change across the concession. A land-cover baseline before disturbance gives the reference point, and dated drone surveys compared side by side show where ground has been cut or filled between visits.

The result is a trend in vegetation cover for each rehabilitated area, which your closure and environmental teams can put in front of reviewers. It is not an ecological assessment, a closure certificate or a regulator's sign-off.

For quarries, stockpiles and borrow pits, volumes are calculated from drone elevation models, and an inventory of stockpiles and laydown areas is compared between dates. Volumes support your mine surveyors; they are not a statutory survey.

::::section{id="countries" tone="alt" eyebrow="Mozambique, South Africa, Nigeria" title="Mining rules that shape the survey" lead="Satellite-based surveys need no site visit and no drone flight, and are offered through our country sites across Africa. A summary of public rules for orientation, as of 26 September 2026, not legal advice."}
:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="Mozambique" icon="map" eyebrow="Português · English"}
The new Mining Law approved by the Assembleia da República in May 2026 (Lei de Minas n.º 7/2026) reserves areas for artisanal and small-scale mining and creates a community fund ([AIM](https://aimnews.org/2026/05/08/ar-aprova-lei-de-minas-que-proibe-venda-de-produtos-minerais-nao-processados/)), so rights holders need to know where surface activity sits relative to their title. Resettlement for expansions follows [Decreto n.º 31/2012](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/644941485846310129/pdf/SFG2931-REVISED-RP-PORTUGUESE-P161351-Box402883B-PUBLIC-Disclosed-2-1-2017.pdf). In remote and insecure areas, satellite-first work lets a baseline start without a site visit, and we never publish site maps. Reports can be delivered in Portuguese.
:::
:::card{title="South Africa" icon="map" eyebrow="English"}
Mining rights are granted under the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act (MPRDA). The Department of Water and Sanitation has called on mines to register their tailings dams on its dam-safety database ([DWS](https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/water-and-sanitation-calls-mines-register-tailing-dams-its-database-ensure)), and closure is funded under the NEMA Financial Provisioning Regulations, 2015, whose compliance date for mining-right holders has been extended ([statement](https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/minister-barbara-creecy-grants-further-extension-holders-mineral-and)). Many large mines fly their own drones under their own SACAA operator certificates, so analysis of your own imagery is usually the place to start.
:::
:::card{title="Nigeria" icon="map" eyebrow="English"}
Mineral titles are administered by the Mining Cadastre Office under the Nigerian Minerals and Mining Act 2007, and land for mining can be taken under the [Land Use Act](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/view2.php?sn=228) (s.28). Drone work needs an NCAA operator certificate under [Nig.CARs Part 21](https://ncaa.gov.ng/media/wabn2axn/nigcars-part-21-remotely-piloted-aircraft-system.pdf) with security clearance built into the process, and many mineral areas are hard to reach, so remote, satellite-first work applies. The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 can apply to imagery that shows identifiable homes.
:::
