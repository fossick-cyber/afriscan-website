faq:
  - q: Is a household estimate a census?
    a: No. Households are estimated from structure counts using stated persons-per-household assumptions drawn from national census data, and every report sets the assumptions out. It is a planning figure, not a count of people.
  - q: Can your maps be used for enforcement or evictions?
    a: We map land for planning and service delivery. We do not decide whether a structure is lawful, and our work is not designed for enforcement or evictions; decisions about occupation rest with the authorities and the courts.
  - q: Can you map damage straight after a cyclone?
    a: Post-event mapping depends on imagery captured after the event. Radar can map the flooded area even under cloud; checks of visible damage need optical or drone imagery captured once the weather allows. It is not an emergency or early-warning service.
  - q: Can we use our own aerial imagery?
    a: Yes. If your department already holds orthophotos or satellite scenes, we run the same structure and change analysis on them. Results depend on the imagery's quality and resolution.

::::section{id="problem" eyebrow="The problem, in your words" title="Planning needs to know what is on the ground now"}
Municipal land teams, settlement-upgrading programmes, disaster-recovery units and energy-access programmes all start from the same question: how many buildings are there, where, and how is that changing? Official maps are often years old in fast-growing places, and field enumeration is slow and costly to repeat.

:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="“Our base map is years out of date.”" icon="map"}
Settlements have grown since the last aerial survey. Planners need a current structure layer to load into their own GIS.
:::
:::card{title="“Which areas flood, and what was damaged?”" icon="water"}
Recovery teams need to know which structures sit in ground that floods, and what visibly changed after the event.
:::
:::card{title="“How many households does each village have?”" icon="houses"}
Energy-access and service programmes need honest estimates, with the assumptions stated, before the field teams go out.
:::
:::

::::section{id="municipal-land" tone="alt" eyebrow="Municipal land and settlement planning" title="A current structure layer, and what changed since"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
We deliver a GIS layer of structures across a district, municipality or imagery survey, ready for your own mapping system, with reviewer-led mapping where automatic detection is not enough, such as dense informal settlement or mixed tree cover. Structures inside and around public land parcels can be listed with their distance to the boundary.

Between dated surveys, new and removed structures are flagged and confirmed by a reviewer, and the area can be re-surveyed on a schedule agreed with you. A trend of built-up land over the years shows where settlement is growing fastest, estimated households support service planning, and a land-cover baseline adds built-up land, cropland, bare ground, water and tree cover. If your department holds its own aerial imagery, we work from that.

::::section{id="disaster-risk" eyebrow="Disaster risk and recovery" title="Before the season, and after the event"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Before the rainy or cyclone season, flood-exposure screening highlights the structures, roads and sites in ground seen as water in past satellite records, in low-lying areas or at river crossings, and terrain screening flags steep and erosion-prone ground. A structure layer and estimated households for the exposed areas support preparedness planning.

After a flood or cyclone, radar imagery maps the flooded area even under cloud, and a reviewer checks structures and assets before and after the event on optical or drone imagery captured once the weather allows.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="Screening and checks, not modelling"}
Flood-exposure screening is not a hydraulic model, an official flood line or an insurance rating. Post-event work depends on imagery captured after the event, covers visible damage only, and is not an emergency or early-warning service.
:::

### Public land, estates and free zones {#public-land-estates}

For industrial estates, special economic zones, free zones, ports and public land banks, we list the structures inside the boundary and in a ring around it, flag change at the perimeter between dated surveys, with fence lines checked by a reviewer on drone imagery, and document the land's history from archived imagery. It is a land record for planning and management, not a title or valuation check.
::::
::::col
### Health campaign microplanning {#microplanning}

Structure layers and estimated households help health programmes plan campaigns, teams and supplies for each settlement. We combine our reviewed structure layer with open population data, such as WorldPop and, in Nigeria, GRID3, credited as their terms require, and state the assumptions. It is not a census, and a structure's category describes what the imagery shows, not what the building is used for.

Municipalities procure under the Municipal Finance Management Act, and settlement-upgrading programmes, including those supported by the Housing Development Agency, need current structure layers and household estimates. [GRID3 Nigeria](https://grid3.org/geospatial-data-nigeria) already publishes open settlement data; what we add is a reviewed structure layer for your area, change between dates and stated assumptions.

### What we do

:::checklist
- Map structures and land cover for planning and service delivery
- Estimate households from structure counts, with the assumptions stated
- Screen flood exposure and check visible damage after events
- Flag change between dated surveys, confirmed by a reviewer
:::
::::
::::col
### What we don't do

:::checklist{tone="no"}
- Take a census or enumerate households
- Decide whether a structure is lawful, or support evictions or enforcement
- Run an emergency, early-warning or public-safety service
- Verify title, tenure or building use
:::
