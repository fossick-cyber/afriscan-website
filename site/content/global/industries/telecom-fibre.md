faq:
  - q: Can you detect cable theft, cuts or vandalism?
    a: "No. Satellites and drones see the ground, not the cable, and they cannot show theft, vandalism or who was on the route. What imagery does show is land change: fresh excavation, trenches, spoil heaps and earthworks on or near your route, and new structures, tracks and cleared ground. We flag those between surveys so your patrols know where to check. What happened, and why, is for your teams and the authorities to establish."
  - q: Our fibre runs in road reserves and power-line servitudes. Can you measure against those widths?
    a: Yes. We measure from the route you supply, and the bands can follow the duct or wayleave width, the road reserve the route sits in, or the servitude of the line it runs beside, plus a wider band to watch. Because distances are measured to your route file, a route drawn from as-built records gives the most useful register; where the alignment is uncertain, a wider band helps.
  - q: How often can a route be re-surveyed?
    a: On a schedule agreed with you, never in real time. We re-survey when new dated imagery of your route is available, and send a notice after each survey saying what changed and where. How often depends on when new imagery is available, on cloud and, for drone checks, on permits, so the cadence is set per project and written into the proposal.
  - q: Does this replace our patrols or our planned-works process?
    a: No. It tells your patrols and planned-works teams where to look first, and gives you a dated record of the ground between their visits. Anything the imagery cannot settle is listed for a check on the ground.
  - q: Can you help plan a new fibre route?
    a: Yes. We count the structures along each route option within the bands you choose, screen each option for steep, erosion-prone and drainage-crossing stretches and for ground seen as water in past satellite records, and fix a dated baseline once the route is chosen. It is an input to your route selection, not an engineering design.
  - q: Do you map the people who live or work along the route?
    a: No. We map structures, excavations, tracks and cleared ground, never people, and we never attribute works to anyone. Whether a structure or a trench is authorised is for you and the authorities to decide.

::::section{id="routes" tone="alt" eyebrow="Buried routes and wayleaves" title="A register of what stands, and what is being dug, along your route"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
We measure from the route you supply, in its local UTM zone, so distances are in true metres. You choose the bands: the duct or wayleave width, the road reserve or servitude the route sits in, and a wider band to see pressure building before it reaches the route. Each structure the review confirms is listed with its distance to the route, its band, its chainage and its coordinates, and each 500 m stretch is rated for **encroachment density**, a count rule that shows where structures cluster so your teams know where to go first. It is not a rating of risk to the cable.

Between dated surveys, the same route is surveyed again. New and removed structures are flagged automatically and confirmed by a reviewer, with before-and-after views of each change. **Fresh digging, trenches, spoil heaps and earthworks on or near the route** are flagged in the same way, with their chainage and distance to the route, together with new tracks across the wayleave and a trend of built-up land around the route that shows where settlement pressure is growing.

Only works visible at the surface can be flagged. Anything under tree canopy or smaller than the imagery can show may be missed, which is why the imagery is chosen to match the features that matter, and why every result is confirmed by a reviewer.
::::
::::col

::::section{id="patrols" eyebrow="Route patrols and planned works" title="A list of places to check, after every survey"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
After each survey, your team receives a notice listing the stretches where the ground has changed: new excavations and earthworks, new structures and new tracks, each with its chainage, distance to the route and coordinates your teams can navigate to. Patrols can start with the stretches that changed instead of working the whole route in order, and the interactive map file opens in a web browser in the field.

Where you already know works are planned along the route, such as a road widening, another utility's trench or a new development beside the reserve, dated imagery before and after the works shows where the ground was opened and what was built. Imagery is captured on particular dates, so the before and after views depend on when new imagery of your route is available; a new capture can be requested, but its date is never guaranteed.

Where a flagged stretch needs more detail, drone checks capture it, subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="What imagery can and cannot see"}
Satellites and drones see the surface: fresh digging, spoil, trenches, new structures, tracks and cleared ground. They cannot see the cable, a cut, theft, vandalism or who was on the route. We flag land change so your teams know where to look. We never follow or identify people or vehicles.
:::

::::section{id="new-routes" tone="alt" eyebrow="New fibre routes" title="Compare route options, then fix a baseline"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
For a new backbone, a metro ring or a last-mile extension, the structures a route avoids are the ones nobody has to negotiate over. We count the structures along each route option within the bands you choose, so planners can weigh options on the land they would affect alongside their engineering and cost criteria.

Each option can be screened for steep and erosion-prone stretches, drainage crossings and ground seen as water in past satellite records. Once the route is chosen, a dated baseline records what stood along it before construction, which your team can compare with the state of the route after the build. Where drone surveys are flown during construction, dated orthophotos compared side by side show where ground has been cut or filled between visits, and revegetation along the reinstated trench line can be tracked between dates.

The comparison is an input to route selection. The terrain work is screening, not a geotechnical study, and construction views are not a certified survey or a valuation of work done.
::::
::::col

- Flood-exposure screening from past satellite water records
- A dated baseline register of the chosen route
- Construction progress from dated drone surveys, subject to approvals
- Revegetation of the reinstated route, tracked between dates

### Tower compounds and exchanges {#tower-sites}

Around tower compounds, exchanges and cabinets the question becomes a boundary: what stands inside the site and in a ring around it, and what has changed at its edge. We list the structures inside and around each site with their distance to the boundary, and map new structures, cleared ground and new tracks at the boundary between dates, with fence lines and gates checked by a reviewer on drone imagery. After a cyclone or flood, a reviewer checks sites and routes before and after the event on imagery captured once the weather allows. Post-event checks cover visible damage only and are not an emergency service.
::::
::::col
### Fibre in shared corridors {#shared-corridors}

Much fibre runs inside land another owner answers for: a road reserve, a power-line servitude or a pipeline right of way. The same register serves both owners, measured against both sets of widths. See how we work in [road and rail reserves](key:rail-roads#reserves), [line servitudes](key:power-utilities#servitudes) and [pipeline rights of way](key:oil-gas#rights-of-way), and where fibre shares a trench with a new pipeline or line, the baseline for that project can include the fibre route.

::::section{id="countries" eyebrow="Country rules" title="The reserves and strips your route sits in" lead="Fibre routes usually sit inside land that a law already reserves for a road or a utility, and those rules decide who may build there and who pays when a route has to move. A summary of public rules for orientation, as of 27 September 2026, not legal advice; the sources are linked."}
| Instrument | What it sets |
|---|---|
| **Mozambique**<br>[Lei n.º 19/97, de 1 de Outubro (Lei de Terras)](https://www.pdul.gov.mz/content/download/486/2635/file/Lei%20de%20Terras.pdf), arts. 8(g) and 9 | A partial protection zone of 50 m on each side of overhead, surface, underground and submarine telecommunications conduits, as for electricity, oil, gas and water, where no land-use right (DUAT) can be acquired, only special licences |
| **Angola**<br>[Lei n.º 9/04, de 9 de Novembro (Lei de Terras)](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/ang49570.pdf), art. 27(7)(g) | Land occupied by telecommunications installations and conductors, as for electricity, water, oil and gas, with a 30 m strip on each side, is listed among the partial reserves |
| **Kenya**<br>[Kenya Roads Act 2007](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2007/2/eng@2025-08-26), ss. 27 and 49 | Utilities in a road reserve, expressly including fibre optic cable, are relocated at no cost to the road authority on its written request (s.27); nothing may be laid on, over or below a road reserve without the authority's written permission (s.49) |
| **Uganda**<br>[Roads Act 2019](https://archive.gazettes.africa/archive/ug/2019/ug-government-gazette-dated-2019-09-25-no-48.pdf), s.16 | A road authority may authorise optic fibre cables in a road reserve in writing; when it needs the reserve, the holder removes them at its own cost, without compensation, after notice |
| **Rwanda**<br>[RURA Regulation No 20/R/ICT/RURA/2026](https://www.rura.rw/fileadmin/user_upload/RURA/Documents/Sectors/ICT/Regulatory_Instruments/ICT_Regulations_and_Guidelines/Regulatio_Governing_Telecommunication__Infrastructure_Sharing_and_Service_provision_in_Rwanda_.pdf), art. 23 | Anyone seeking authorisation to deploy fibre follows the Ministerial Guidelines on Multi-Utility Channels |

Where a route has to move for a road, or a structure stands on a reserve without permission, when things appeared on the ground can matter. A dated register records what stood where on the imagery date; it does not decide who is responsible or who pays.

Satellite-based surveys need no site visit and no drone flight, and are offered through our country sites across Africa. Drone surveys are subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires, and Afridrone is working towards the operator approvals each country requires. The rules are compared in [drone survey rules by country](/drone-regulations) and summarised on [where we work](/countries).
