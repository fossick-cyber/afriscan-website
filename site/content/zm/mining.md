::::section{id="problem" eyebrow="The problem, in your words" title="Consent distances fixed in law, land that keeps changing"}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="“We need consent before we work near those houses.”" icon="houses" eyebrow="MRC Act 2024, s.35"}
A rights holder needs the owner's or occupier's written consent before working within 180 m of an occupied house or building, and the consent of the chief and the local authority on village or customary land. A register of what stands where, and since when, is where those conversations start.
:::
:::card{title="“New houses appear on our exploration licence.”" icon="boundary" eyebrow="MRC Act 2024, s.37(2)"}
An owner or occupier of land under an exploration licence "shall not erect a building or structure on the land without the consent of the holder of the exploration licence". Knowing when each structure first appears keeps later compensation talks grounded.
:::
:::card{title="“Pits keep opening at the edge of the licence area.”" icon="excavation" eyebrow="Artisanal digging"}
Artisanal digging shows up as pits, spoil heaps and water-filled excavations, often along the licence boundary or on old workings. Mapping the ground between dates shows where it is spreading and where it has stopped.
:::
:::card{title="“The expansion footprint needs a baseline.”" icon="calendar" eyebrow="Expansions and resettlement"}
A new pit, dump or tailings facility changes who is affected. A dated record of the structures inside the new footprint is needed before the census and before the cut-off date is announced.
:::

::::section{id="surface-rights" tone="alt" eyebrow="The Minerals Regulation Commission Act 2024" title="Where a rights holder needs consent before working"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
The [Minerals Regulation Commission Act 2024](https://www.parliament.gov.zm/sites/default/files/documents/acts/Act%20No.%2014%20of%202024%20The%20Mineral%20Regulation%20Commission.pdf) (No. 14 of 2024) repeals the Mines and Minerals Development Act 2015 (s.97(1)); licences granted earlier stay valid until they expire (s.97(2)), and the Commission's first licensing committee met on 17 June 2026. Under s.35, a rights holder may not work without written consent:

| Near | Distance | Whose consent |
|---|---|---|
| An inhabited, occupied or temporarily uninhabited house or building | 180 m | The owner or occupier |
| Cleared or cropped land | 45 m | The owner or occupier |
| A cattle dip tank, dam or private water | 90 m | The owner or occupier |
| A building or dam owned by the Republic | 90 m | The appropriate authority |
| Any railway track, or railway land | 100 m | The railway administration |
| City, municipal or district boundaries | 60 m | The local authority |
| Village or customary land | | The chief and the local authority for the district |

The section also covers burial grounds, monuments, Government aerodromes, forests, streets and roads, national parks, game management areas and bird sanctuaries, and land that needs planning permission.
::::
::::col

Section 37(2) adds that landholders under an exploration licence may not build without the licence holder's consent, with the Commission able to grant consent that is unreasonably withheld, and s.40 requires "fair and reasonable compensation" for disturbance and for damage to crops, trees and buildings, with claims within three years. No exploration, mining, processing or gold panning may take place without a right and prior ZEMA approval of the environmental impact assessment (s.10), and artisanal mining is reserved to citizens and co-operatives wholly composed of citizens (s.20). Each of those rules is easier to apply with a dated register of what is on the land. For the full picture of wayleaves, reserves and consents, see [our wayleave guide](/zm/wayleave-guide#mining).

::::section{id="artisanal-digging" tone="alt" eyebrow="Artisanal digging as ground disturbance" title="Pits, spoil and disturbed ground on and around your licence area"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
New pits, spoil heaps, water-filled excavations and disturbed ground inside and around your licence area are mapped from dated imagery, with new sites flagged between dates and confirmed by a reviewer. Tracks leading to them are mapped the same way, so your teams can see how sites are reached and where activity is spreading, moving or stopping. In the rainy season, radar comparisons show larger ground changes even under cloud, and flagged areas are followed up with optical imagery or a drone check.

What we map is the ground. We do not identify, count or follow people, and underground workings and covered shafts are not visible from above. Flagged sites are indications for your teams to check, not proof of anything, and whether and how to act on them is your decision, taken with the authorities and the communities around the licence area. Where formalisation of artisanal and small-scale mining is on the table under the Geological and Minerals Development Act 2025, the same dated maps show where digging has taken place and how the ground has changed.

::::section{id="tailings" eyebrow="Tailings, blast and hazard zones" title="A current list of the structures inside the zones your engineers define"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
You supply the zones: the inundation area from a dam-break study, a blast exclusion radius, a flood line or a safety setback. We list each structure the review confirms inside them, with its location, its distance to the source and its band, and show how settlement inside each zone changes between surveys. Where compounds are dense or roofs are hard to read, reviewers mark structures by hand on high-resolution imagery.

On request, we add estimated households derived from the structure counts, with the assumptions stated, for preparedness planning and engagement. Zones are re-surveyed on the schedule you agree with us, with a notice after each survey saying what changed.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="What this register is, and isn't"}
A list of structures in the zones you define, with the imagery date on every record. It is not a dam-break model, a dam-safety assessment or a statement of conformance with any tailings standard, and it counts structures, not people at risk.

::::section{id="expansions" tone="alt" eyebrow="Expansions and resettlement" title="Record the footprint before the census"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
When a pit, dump, plant or tailings facility expands, we produce a dated record of the structures inside the new footprint, with reviewer categories such as main building, outbuilding, livestock enclosure or under construction, and on request estimated households. We found no stand-alone resettlement statute in Zambia; compensation runs through the Lands Acquisition Act and s.40 of the Minerals Regulation Commission Act, and lender-financed projects add IFC Performance Standard 5. Our record supports the census and asset inventory and the cut-off-date records; it does not replace them, and categories do not establish use, ownership or value.

After the cut-off date, dated re-surveys show what has appeared in the footprint since, and a location check pulls every dated image of a single plot when a claim needs it.
