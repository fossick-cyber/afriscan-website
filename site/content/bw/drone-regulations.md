faq:
  - q: Does a satellite survey need CAAB approval?
    a: No aircraft is flown, so the drone regulations do not come into the satellite work itself. We found no Botswana law restricting the analysis of commercial satellite imagery, and no rule like Mozambique's that makes aerial data State property or needs a defence authorisation for each survey. Your counsel should confirm both points for your project.
  - q: Can a South African or other foreign drone company fly survey work in Botswana?
    a: Not in its own name, on the published texts. Only citizens, lawful residents, Botswana companies with their principal place of business in Botswana and the Government may own a drone (reg. 5); a drone cannot be registered if an unqualified person holds any legal or beneficial interest in it (reg. 7(5)(b)); every drone flown in Botswana must be on the Botswana register (reg. 7(1)); and the RPAS Operator Certificate goes only to an applicant with its principal place of business registered in Botswana (reg. 40). A foreign firm works through a Botswana company, or through a Botswana operator, that meets those rules.
  - q: How far ahead should a drone survey be planned?
    a: An application for an RPAS Operator Certificate is made at least 60 days before the intended operation (reg. 39), and so is an application for an exemption (regs 141 to 144). Every commercial operation then needs its own CAAB authorisation, with the security review of the camera, and security vetting is part of registration and licensing. Ask the operator for its dates before you fix a programme.
  - q: Can a drone fly along our power line?
    a: Only with planning. The 2024 regulations keep a drone 50 m from any structure not under the control of the person in charge of it unless CAAB approves otherwise (reg. 24), and CAAB's 2016 bye-law, still published, lists flights within 200 m of any power line among its restricted areas. Plan the flight with the line's owner and ask CAAB in writing for the permission the job needs, or start from satellite imagery.
  - q: Do we need permission to take off from tribal land or a farm?
    a: Yes. A drone may not be launched or recovered from any public or private property without the consent of the relevant authority (reg. 100), CAAB's checklist asks commercial operators for the property owner's letter of authorisation, and the pilot or owner must notify the appropriate authorities and the people around the area before starting.
  - q: Is CAAB's 2016 bye-law still in force?
    a: That has not been settled publicly. CAAB still publishes Bye-Law 07 of 2016, and S.I. No. 71 of 2024 neither mentions nor revokes it, but a 2022 circular said its list applied until 6 June 2023 and CAAB's current drone page leaves the list out. Until CAAB confirms the position in writing, plan as if the restricted areas apply and ask for written special permission where the work needs it.
  - q: Can a drone follow an interconnector across the border?
    a: No. CAAB's page says a drone registered in Botswana is not permitted to cross international boundaries, and a flight into or out of Botswana needs the other State's authorisation as well as CAAB's (reg. 46(2) and (3)). Survey each side of a border under its own country's rules.

:::card{title="The owner" icon="building" eyebrow="Reg. 5"}
Only a citizen, a person lawfully resident in Botswana, a company incorporated in Botswana with its principal place of business there, or the Government may own a drone ([S.I. No. 71 of 2024](https://www.caab.co.bw/wp-content/uploads/Civil-Aviation-Remotely-Piloted-Aircraft-Regulations-2024.pdf)).

Registered by CAAB, with a certificate of registration carried on every operation and an identification plate "made of stainless steel or other fireproof material".
:::
:::card{title="The operator" icon="file-check" eyebrow="Regs 37 to 45"}
An **RPAS Operator Certificate (ROC)** for any commercial operation, valid for 12 months and applied for at least 60 days ahead.
:::
:::card{title="Each operation" icon="calendar" eyebrow="Reg. 46"}
A CAAB **authorisation** for each commercial operation, with the camera's use sent "for Security review and approval" (reg. 71(4)(d)).
:::
:::card{title="Each pilot" icon="user-check" eyebrow="Regs 47 to 63 and 105 to 115"}
A CAAB **remote pilot licence**, with the ratings the flight needs and a current medical certificate.
:::
:::card{title="The people" icon="shield" eyebrow="Reg. 98"}
**Security vetting** of owners and licence applicants by "competent security agencies", and background checks every 24 months on everyone who deploys, handles or stores the drones.
:::

The regulations apply to "all persons operating or maintaining a RPAS registered in Botswana wherever they may be and any RPAS operating in Botswana" (reg. 3). They were made by the Minister of Transport and Public Works on 14 June 2024 under section 89 of the Civil Aviation Act, whose regulation-making powers the [Civil Aviation (Amendment) Act, 2022](https://www.caab.co.bw/wp-content/uploads/Civil-Aviation-Amendment-Act-2022.pdf) extended to "the operation of remotely piloted aircraft", and published in the [Extraordinary Gazette of 14 June 2024](https://www.caab.co.bw/wp-content/uploads/Extraordinary-Gazette-14th-June-2024.pdf).

**Registration in practice.** Drones are registered "through our Personnel Licensing Office at Sir Seretse Khama International Airport", where the drone and its documents are inspected; "The client will then await clearance from the security vetting department" ([CAAB, RPA – Drone Registration](https://www.caab.co.bw/rpa-drone-registration/)). CAAB is also the air navigation service provider and an airport operator; the Minister of Transport and Infrastructure told Parliament in March 2026 that the sector "will devolve to have the regulator independent from operations" ([Committee of Supply speech](https://www.parliament.gov.bw/documents/BUDGET-2026-27-COMMITTE-OF-SUPPLY-SPEECH-2026-27-Transport-and-Infrastructure_10_25_55_04_08_2026.pdf)).

::::section{id="operator" eyebrow="The operator" title="The RPAS Operator Certificate"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
"An operator of a remotely piloted aircraft shall not engage in commercial RPAS operations unless the operator holds a valid RPA Operator Certificate (ROC) issued by the Authority" (reg. 38(1)). A commercial operation is one done for remuneration and offered to the public, or done under a contract for a customer who has no control over the operator (reg. 2), so a survey for a client is commercial work (Category C under reg. 4(3)).

**The application** is made on Form A of the Fifth Schedule, at least 60 days before the date of the intended operation (reg. 39). The certificate states the types of operation authorised, the drone models and "the Authorized areas of operation".

**Conditions of issue** (reg. 40). The applicant must have its principal place of business registered in Botswana, a security clearance issued by the appropriate authority, and an approved Aircraft Operator Security Programme.

**Validity.** Twelve months from issue or renewal (reg. 41).

**What the operator keeps up.** An accountable manager and competent staff with recurrent training (reg. 44), an operations manual that follows the Third Schedule (reg. 45), a safety management system (reg. 38(4)) and records (reg. 133).
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="note" title="Security duties that come with the certificate"}
- Operator security procedures for every operation (reg. 71(1)).
- A security coordinator, secure storage of the drones and security-awareness training (reg. 99).
- A security threat assessment by the security agencies, including "Interpol and international databases, terrorist watch lists" (reg. 98(3)).
- Background and criminal-record checks every 24 months on all personnel who deploy, handle or store the drones (reg. 98(6)).
- Third-party insurance "in force" for every operation, at a minimum sum CAAB determines unless it dispenses with the cover for the class and category, with the certificate available at the operator's operational management (reg. 146).

::::section{id="foreign-operators" tone="alt" eyebrow="For foreign and cross-border buyers" title="Why a foreign drone operator cannot fly commercially in its own name"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
On the published texts, commercial drone work in Botswana is done by a Botswana operator:

- ownership is limited to citizens, lawful residents, Botswana companies with their principal place of business in Botswana, and the Government (reg. 5), and a drone cannot be registered if "an unqualified person holds any legal or beneficial interest by way of ownership" (reg. 7(5)(b));
- every drone operated within Botswana must be on the Botswana register (reg. 7(1));
- the ROC needs a principal place of business registered in Botswana, a security clearance and an approved security programme (reg. 40);
- CAAB's own page says operators "will be citizens or residents";
- an air service licence, including an aerial work licence, goes to a Botswana statutory corporation, a Government department, or a company incorporated under the Companies Act with a place of business in Botswana ([S.I. No. 64 of 2012](https://www.caab.co.bw/wp-content/uploads/Licensing-of-Air-Services-Regulations-2012.pdf), reg. 33(1)).

The Civil Aviation Act provides an operating permit for aerial work "by a foreign based operator" (s.49(1)(d)). Whether it can be used for drones, given regulations 5 and 7, is a question for CAAB.

Non-citizens or non-residents who wish to bring a drone into Botswana to fly it must apply to CAAB in writing with seven days' notice ([CAAB, Legal Requirements](https://www.caab.co.bw/wp-content/uploads/REMOTELY-PILOTED-AIRCRAFT-IN-BOTSWANA-Legal-Requirements.pdf)). That is a visitor's route, not a route for commercial work.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="legal" title="Foreign pilots"}
A pilot with an ICAO Annex 1 licence from another State can ask CAAB for a validation certificate, valid for up to 12 months and never beyond the foreign licence, or for a conversion. The conditions include English at level 4, a class 3 or class 1 medical, knowledge of air law and a skills test (regs 93 to 96).

A validated pilot still flies for a Botswana operator with its own ROC and authorisations.

::::section{id="aircraft" eyebrow="Each drone" title="Registration, classes and equipment"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
"A person shall not operate a remotely piloted aircraft within Botswana unless the remotely piloted aircraft has been registered by the Authority and a certificate of registration has been issued" (reg. 7(1)). The application (reg. 8) carries the First Schedule form, "evidence of ownership (such as a bill of sale)", the fee and the documents for security vetting. The form asks for the equipment fitted or carried, the nature of operations, such as survey, and the area of operations, and its notes ask for insurance cover and a copy of the operational procedures manual.

The certificate "shall not be transferable" and is carried during operations (reg. 10), and the identification plate is stainless steel or another fireproof material (reg. 12). CAAB may require a drone of a given class and category with a type certificate to obtain a certificate of airworthiness, valid for 12 months (reg. 14).

**Import.** A drone or a drone component may be imported only from a supplier or vendor authorised by CAAB (reg. 6(1)); we found no published list of authorised suppliers. Exporting a Botswana-registered drone needs written notice and a deregistration certificate (reg. 6(2)).

**Radios.** BOCRA type-approves radio equipment under section 84 of the Communications Regulatory Authority Act, 2012 ([BOCRA](https://www.bocra.org.bw/type-approval)), and the authorisation request for an operation includes a copy of the aircraft radio station licence, "if applicable" (reg. 46(6)(h)).

::::section{id="pilots" tone="alt" eyebrow="Each pilot" title="The remote pilot licence, and its two sets of requirements"}
The regulations set out pilot licensing twice. Part V, "Licensing Requirements for Remote Pilots Engaged in International BVLOS Operations", contains regulation 49; the general licensing rules are regulations 105 to 115.

| | Reg. 49 (Part V) | Reg. 107 (general set) |
|---|---|---|
| Age | 18 or over | 18 or over |
| Medical | A current class 3 or class 1 medical certificate | A class 3 or class 1 medical |
| Language | At least level 4 English language proficiency | Not mentioned |
| Radio | A radiotelephony licence | Not mentioned |
| Training | An approved training course | An approved training course |
| Tests | Knowledge and skills tests | Knowledge and skills tests |

The published texts do not say which set CAAB applies to a pilot flying domestic survey work within visual line of sight. Plan for the stricter set, and ask CAAB. Ratings cover BVLOS, extended VLOS, VLOS and instructor privileges (reg. 49(3)); night flying needs dual night instruction (reg. 63(2)). A licence is valid for two years (regs 55 and 114), a student licence is needed for training (regs 48 and 106), and the security-vetting documents go with the application (regs 50(2)(i) and 108(2)(h)).

::::section{id="operation" eyebrow="Each operation" title="The authorisation, the camera review and the take-off site"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
The holder of an ROC may not undertake commercial operations except with an authorisation issued by CAAB, and anyone who intends to conduct an operation must seek it before the operation starts (reg. 46). The request includes:

- copies of the registration, airworthiness certificate, ROC, remote pilot licence and aircraft radio station licence, if applicable;
- the intended operation: dates, route, levels and duration;
- communications and navigation equipment, the command-and-control link and detect-and-avoid capabilities;
- emergency procedures;
- confirmation of compliance with the aviation security regulations;
- a description of the payload;
- proof of adequate insurance.

"Commercial operators requesting to operate with a camera shall be required to include details of the camera usage in the application for Security review and approval" (reg. 71(4)(d)).
::::
::::col
**The take-off and landing site.** A drone "shall not be launched or recovered from any public or private property without the consent of the relevant Authority", and "An RPAS pilot or the owner shall seek permission or notify the appropriate authorities, as well as people around the area before starting the operations" (reg. 100). CAAB's checklist asks commercial operators for a letter of authorisation from the property owner ([CAAB checklist](https://www.caab.co.bw/wp-content/uploads/CAAB-CL-PEL-61.02.19-Remotely-Piloted-Aircraft-Certificate-Issue.pdf)).

**Before each flight** (reg. 30), the pilot assesses the local weather, the airspace, "the location of persons and property on the surface" and other ground hazards, briefs the crew, checks the links and carries enough power for the flight and at least five minutes more.

Along a line that crosses tribal land, state land and freehold farms, the consents are a deliverable in their own right. Agree at the start who obtains them: the asset owner usually knows the Land Boards and landowners.

::::section{id="rules" tone="alt" eyebrow="The flight" title="Operating rules that shape a corridor survey" lead="S.I. No. 71 of 2024. Several rules have an approval route; none of them can be waived by the client."}
| Rule | What the regulations say | Reg. |
|---|---|---|
| **Height and distance** | Not above 400 ft above ground level, and not within 50 m of any person, vessel, vehicle or structure "which is not under the control of the person in charge of the RPAS". Private and commercial operations may use higher heights and other lateral distances where CAAB approves | 24(1)–(2) |
| **Weather** | Visual meteorological conditions only | 24(3) |
| **Beyond visual line of sight** | Not permitted by default; a drone flown BVLOS must carry a detect-and-avoid system. CAAB's page says BVLOS is possible "under special approval" | 24(3)–(4) |
| **Line of sight** | VLOS is direct unaided visual contact. Extended VLOS: below 400 ft, with an observer keeping visual contact, up to 1,000 m from the pilot. Restricted VLOS: within 500 m of the pilot and below the highest obstacle within 300 m. CAAB's page: "no further than 500m from the Operator" | 2, 26 |
| **Night** | Not at night unless CAAB authorises it | 25 |
| **Aerodromes** | Reg. 21(2): at least 10 km from the centre of any aerodrome. Reg. 35: not within 10 km of code C to F aerodromes or 7 km of code A and B aerodromes, on approach and take-off paths, near navigation aids or in the aerodrome traffic zone, except with the written permission of the aerodrome owner or operator and the air navigation service provider and CAAB's approval | 21(2), 35 |
| **Controlled airspace** | Prior authorisation from the air traffic control unit with jurisdiction, and two-way communication with the air navigation service provider | 21(1) |
| **Prohibited and restricted areas** | Never in prohibited areas; restricted and danger areas, or any other area CAAB notifies, only with CAAB's written permission and conditions | 20 |
| **Crowds** | Not over an open-air assembly of people not taking part in the operation | 29 |
| **Borders** | CAAB: a drone registered in Botswana "is not permitted to cross International Boundaries". A flight out of Botswana needs the other State's authorisation, and a flight into it CAAB's | 46(2)–(3) |
| **In the air** | One pilot, one drone (27); yield the right of way to all aircraft and vehicles (28); command and control at all times (33); incidents reported under the safety-management and accident-investigation regulations (31) | 27–33 |

Regulations 21(2) and 35 sit side by side in the text, one without an exception and one with a permission route: ask CAAB how they apply to your route. CAAB lists Sir Seretse Khama (Gaborone), Maun, P. G. Matante (Francistown) and Kasane as international airports, and also runs Selebi-Phikwe and Ghanzi; a long line can pass within 10 km of several. The prohibited, restricted and danger areas are published in CAAB's aeronautical information publication, which [CAAB's Aeronautical Information Services](https://www.caab.co.bw/aip-aip-sup-airac/) sell by subscription.

**What this means along a line or boundary.** Standard work is a series of VLOS legs of up to about 500 m, below 400 ft, stepping along the route, with the 50 m distance from structures outside the operation's control unless CAAB approves otherwise. Long flights beyond visual line of sight depend on a special approval and detect-and-avoid equipment. The ROC, the authorisation for each operation and the camera's security review all belong in the programme from the start.

::::section{id="bye-law" eyebrow="Older CAAB restrictions" title="The 2016 bye-law: still published, status to confirm" lead="CAAB's Bye-Law 07 of 2016 on remotely operated aircraft, made with the Minister's approval under section 8(2) of the Civil Aviation Act, lists restricted areas that the 2024 regulations do not repeat. CAAB's 2022 circular GC-PEL-035 restates the list. CAAB's current drone page and its 2023 information sheet leave it out."}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
### The list (para. 5.9(a))

No person shall fly a drone:

- "Within a controlled airspace unless with the specific permission of the relevant ATC authority and then only if two way communication with that authority can be maintained";
- "Within a 3km radius of an unmanned airfield unless the operator can monitor the unmanned airfields frequency of 125.5 MHz";
- "Over national game parks and wild life sanctuaries";
- over any tourism facility, such as hotels, lodges and campsites;
- "Over any wildlife in a manner in which the RPA may disturb such wildlife";
- **"Within a lateral distance of 200m from any Power Line"**;
- across an international boundary, if it is registered in Botswana;
- over prohibited and restricted airspace specified in the AIP;
- to take off, land or fly over any major public roads, or over moving traffic;
- adjacent to or above a prison, police station, crime scene, court of law, or Government facilities, including military and security installations;
- over private property without the specific permission of the owner;
- "Over built up areas";
- over public gatherings, or in any way that might cause injury to people or damage to property on the ground should the drone fail.

Special permission for aerial work over these areas is requested in writing from CAAB or the relevant authority, giving the registration number, the date and time and the reason (para. 5.10).
::::
::::col
### Does it still apply?

**Signs that it has lapsed.** The 2022 circular said its restrictions were "effective immediately until 6th June 2023", when applicants and certificate holders were to comply with newer personnel-licensing regulations. S.I. No. 71 of 2024 does not mention the bye-law, and its savings clause (reg. 149) keeps only licences, certificates and authorisations already issued. CAAB's current drone page omits the list.

**Signs that it still applies.** The bye-law was made under section 8(2) of the Act, no instrument we have seen revokes it, and CAAB still publishes it. Regulation 148(8) of the 2024 regulations makes a contravention of "any provision in any document issued or published by the Authority other than an advisory document" an offence.

**How to plan.** Until CAAB confirms the position in writing, treat the 200 m power-line distance and the park, lodge, built-up-area, major-road, Government-facility and private-property restrictions as applying, and ask for written special permission where the work needs them.

CAAB's current page and its 2023 sheet repeat other parts of the bye-law, such as a 20 kg limit, flights within 500 m of the operator and below 400 ft, no night flights, and Police and D.I.S. clearance. Where they differ from S.I. No. 71 of 2024, the later regulations, made by the Minister, are the ones to cite, and CAAB the one to ask.

::::section{id="sensitive-sites" tone="alt" eyebrow="Sensitive sites" title="Power lines, mines, parks, depots, roads and borders"}
:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="Power lines and substations" icon="power"}
The 50 m distance from structures outside the operation's control (reg. 24) and the bye-law's 200 m from any power line both bear on a line inspection. In practice a drone check of a line is planned with the line's owner, and CAAB is asked to approve the distances or to grant special permission. Ask CAAB before planning flights near substations and power stations.
:::
:::card{title="Mines" icon="mine"}
We found no drone rule specific to mines. The mine's site rules and consent apply, through reg. 100 and the property owner's letter, and where a lease area takes in a game park or wildlife sanctuary, the bye-law's park restrictions may apply too.
:::
:::card{title="Parks and wildlife areas" icon="tree"}
The bye-law bars flights over national game parks and wildlife sanctuaries, over tourism facilities and over wildlife in a way that may disturb it. Ask CAAB and the Department of Wildlife and National Parks before planning a flight in or near one.
:::
:::card{title="Fuel depots and security installations" icon="shield"}
The bye-law's restriction on Government facilities, "including, military and security installations", and any prohibited or restricted airspace in the AIP apply. Treat fuel depots and military sites as sensitive and plan with CAAB.
:::
:::card{title="Roads" icon="route"}
The bye-law bars take-off, landing and flight over major public roads and over moving traffic. Lines that share road servitudes are therefore harder to fly than lines across open land.
:::
:::card{title="Borders" icon="flag"}
No cross-border flights by Botswana-registered drones. Border areas are sensitive: plan any flight near a border with CAAB, and survey each side under its own country's rules.

::::section{id="aerial-work" eyebrow="Open questions" title="Aerial work permits, and the points to raise with CAAB"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
**Aerial work for hire.** The Civil Aviation Act defines aerial work to include "photography, surveying, observation and patrol" (s.2), and section 50(1) says: "A person shall not perform aerial work on a flight for hire or reward unless the person holds a permit issued in terms of this Act" ([Civil Aviation Act, 2011](https://www.caab.co.bw/wp-content/uploads/Act.pdf)). Under S.I. No. 64 of 2012, an aerial work licence needs an aerial work operator's certificate and liability insurance (reg. 35), with a permit for each flight or series of flights (reg. 36). The public procurement code for aerial work, 142-02, asks suppliers for a CAAB "Air Operator Certificate" ([PPRA](https://ipms.ppadb.co.bw/files/ipms/downloads/menu/1_List_of_Registration_Codes_PPRA.pdf)).

Whether CAAB also requires these of drone operators, or treats the ROC and the authorisation of each operation as enough, is not settled in the published texts. Ask CAAB, and ask the operator which of them it has for your job.
::::
::::col
:::details{summary="Points in the 2024 text to raise with CAAB"}
- Regulation 21(2) sets a flat 10 km distance from any aerodrome; regulation 35 sets 10 km or 7 km, with a permission route.
- Pilot licensing appears twice, in regulations 47 to 63 (Part V, international BVLOS) and 105 to 115, with different requirements.
- Regulation 55 makes a licence valid for two years, while regulation 56 allows renewal for five.
- The certificate in the Sixth Schedule (CAAB Form AIR 269) still cites the "Civil Aviation (Remotely Piloted Aircraft Systems) Regulations, 2021".
- The arrangement of regulations lists a "Domestic BVLOS" Part VI and regulation 64, but in the body Part VI covers private, recreational and sports operations and regulation 64 is "Private RPAS operations".
- Regulation 26(2) defines "unaided visual contact" by listing binoculars, telescopic, night vision and other visual enhancing equipment.
- Which category, private or commercial, applies to a utility or mine flying its own drones.
- Whether CAAB keeps lists of authorised drone suppliers (reg. 6) and of operators with an ROC: we found none published.

::::section{id="penalties" tone="alt" eyebrow="Enforcement" title="Penalties"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
**Under the 2024 regulations** (reg. 148), operating so as to endanger "the safety of the air space, other aircraft, persons and property on the ground", operating without authorisation, failing to display the registration number, operating an unregistered drone, or an operator failing its obligations, is punishable by a fine, imprisonment for up to six months, or both. Contraventions with no specified penalty, and contraventions of documents CAAB issues or publishes other than advisory ones, carry a fine, imprisonment for up to 10 years, or both (reg. 148(7)–(8)).
::::
::::col
**Under the Act**, aerial work without a permit carries a fine, imprisonment for up to 10 years, or both (s.50(3)), and CAAB may direct an owner, operator or pilot not to fly and take the steps needed to detain the aircraft (s.59). **Administratively**, CAAB may revoke or suspend any document and "prevent any person or RPA from flying" (reg. 17(4)), and refusing a drug or alcohol test can bar a person from licences for up to one year (reg. 136(2)).

::::section{id="data" eyebrow="Beyond aviation" title="Privacy, personal data, land surveys and satellite work"}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="Privacy under the regulations" icon="lock"}
A camera operator must "operate it in a responsible way to respect the privacy of others". Regulation 101 also bars using a drone to observe a person without their consent or private property without the owner's consent, and to photograph or film an individual without consent in order to publish it, with exceptions for newsgathering and public events. Along a corridor, that means the asset owner's and landowners' consent, flights for land and asset mapping, and no published images of identifiable people.
:::
:::card{title="The Data Protection Act, 2024" icon="file-text"}
Imagery of homes linked to names, resettlement census lists and grievance records are personal data. The Minister for State President told Parliament that implementation of the Data Protection Act started on 14 January 2025, and that the Information and Data Protection Commission would publish its address once it has permanent premises ([BOPA, 29 July 2025](https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/87795)). Have counsel check its duties for your project files.
:::
:::card{title="Orthophotos are not cadastral surveys" icon="ruler"}
The Department of Surveys and Mapping regulates cadastral surveying and approves cadastral surveys to facilitate the registration of title under the Land Survey Act (Cap. 33:01) ([Ministry of Lands and Agriculture](https://www.gov.bw/ministries/ministry-lands-and-agriculture)). Drone and satellite registers support that work; they do not replace it.
:::
:::card{title="Satellite work" icon="satellite"}
No aircraft is flown, so the drone regulations do not apply to satellite screening. We found no Botswana law restricting the analysis of commercial satellite imagery, and no Botswana rule that makes aerial data State property or requires a defence authorisation for each survey, as Mozambique's law does. Counsel should confirm both points for your project.
:::

::::section{id="checklist" tone="alt" eyebrow="For clients" title="Checklist: before you commission a drone survey in Botswana" lead="Ask any operator, including one we propose, for each of these before the first flight."}
:::steps{style="list"}
:::step{title="A current CAAB RPAS Operator Certificate"}
With the types of operation, the drone models and the authorised areas it covers, and its 12-month expiry date.
:::
:::step{title="CAAB's authorisation for this operation"}
Including the security review of the camera's use, and any approval of reduced distances, BVLOS or night flying that the plan relies on.
:::
:::step{title="Each drone registered in Botswana"}
The certificate of registration and the fireproof identification plate for every drone on the plan, and a certificate of airworthiness where CAAB requires one, as it does for Class 3.
:::
:::step{title="A CAAB licence for each pilot"}
A CAAB remote pilot licence with the right ratings and a current medical, or a CAAB validation of a foreign licence.
:::
:::step{title="Any aerial work permit CAAB requires"}
Ask CAAB, and the operator, whether the Act's section 50 permit or an aerial work licence applies to the job.
:::
:::step{title="Air traffic and aerodrome permissions"}
Within 10 km (or 7 km) of an aerodrome, or in controlled airspace.
:::
:::step{title="Consent for every take-off and landing site"}
Written consent from the asset owner and the landowner or Land Board, and notice to the local authorities and the people in the area.
:::
:::step{title="Special permission for the bye-law's restricted areas"}
Within 200 m of power lines, over parks, lodges, built-up areas and major roads, and near Government facilities, until CAAB confirms whether the list still applies.
:::
:::step{title="Insurance"}
Third-party liability cover in force for the operation, with the certificate available.
:::
:::step{title="Security vetting"}
Of the crew, with the operator's security coordinator and storage arrangements.
:::
:::step{title="Data"}
Where imagery and derived data are stored, who can see them, how people are masked, and how the Data Protection Act, 2024 is met.
