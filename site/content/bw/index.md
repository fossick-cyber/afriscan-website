faq:
  - q: Which servitude widths do you measure in Botswana?
    a: The ones that apply to your line. The only widths we found published are the typical designs for BPC's World Bank-financed 66 kV and 33 kV lines, with permanent rights of way of 30 m and 15 m, which the framework says "will be further defined" as the project is implemented ([ESMF, May 2024](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/099052224012584077/pdf/P18122113057240211a4a61be136f2f526c.pdf)). For 132, 220 and 400 kV lines, send the width per line or the servitude polygons from your GIS. Up to six widths can be reported in one survey.
  - q: Does a satellite survey need CAAB approval?
    a: No aircraft is flown, so satellite-based servitude and right-of-way screening needs no drone permit and is available across Botswana. We found no Botswana law restricting the analysis of commercial satellite imagery; counsel should confirm it for your project. Drone detail, where you want it, is planned separately and is subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires.
  - q: Can you tell us whether a structure is lawful?
    a: No. We map what stands where, and when it first appears in the imagery. Whether a structure on tribal land, state land or a freehold farm is allowed, or predates the servitude, is for you, the Land Board or other land authority and the courts to decide. The register gives them a dated, located list to work from.
  - q: Is the register a cadastral survey?
    a: No. The Department of Surveys and Mapping regulates cadastral surveying and approves cadastral surveys for the registration of title under the Land Survey Act (Cap. 33:01) ([Ministry of Lands and Agriculture](https://www.gov.bw/ministries/ministry-lands-and-agriculture)). Our registers are monitoring and planning records built from imagery. They show your surveyor and your wayleave officers where to look.
  - q: Can you detect cable theft or vandalism?
    a: No. Satellites cannot see theft, vandalism or people. We map land change, such as fresh digging, spoil, new tracks, cleared ground and new structures along a line or buried route, so that your patrols and land teams know where to look first.
  - q: Who sees our routes and the results?
    a: Only the contacts you name. Registers describe structures, not people, and carry no names. We never publish maps of your assets or of the settlements around them without your written permission, and sensitive sites are masked in the deliverables.

::::section{id="rules" eyebrow="Already on paper" title="The rules are on the ground" lead="Botswana's servitudes, road reserves and mining licences come with rules about what may stand on the land. Those rules only help if someone can see what is there, and when it arrived."}
:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="Power lines: grazing, not buildings" icon="power" eyebrow="Servitude use"}
On the typical designs for BPC's World Bank-financed 66 kV and 33 kV lines, the permanent right of way is 30 m and 15 m wide, and its permitted use is "Grazing. No cultivation or built infrastructure permitted." ([ESMF, Table 2-1](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/099052224012584077/pdf/P18122113057240211a4a61be136f2f526c.pdf)). BPC's technical-services desk takes requests about new developments that encroach on its servitudes ([BPC](https://www.bpc.bw/technical-services/)).
:::
:::card{title="Road reserves: a wayleave for each service" icon="route" eyebrow="Department of Roads"}
Placing power, water or communication lines inside a road reserve needs an "Access Control to Road Reserve Space (Wayleave)" permit, and the A1 drawings sent with the application must show the utilities already in the reserve or close to it ([gov.bw](https://www.gov.bw/transport/access-control-road-reserve-space-wayleave)).
:::
:::card{title="Mining: surface rights first" icon="mine" eyebrow="Mining licence"}
A mining licence application needs surface rights from the land authority or landowner, with a stamped sketch plan showing coordinates, environmental authorisation from the Department of Environmental Affairs and archaeological clearance ([gov.bw](https://www.gov.bw/mining/mining-license-application)). The Minister of Minerals and Energy says the Mines and Minerals (Amendment) Act, 2024 came into force in October 2025 with revised penalties ([BOPA, 25 August 2026](https://dailynews.gov.bw/news-detail/92924)).
:::
:::

BPC's integrated report for the year to 31 March 2024 records that "Wayleave risks and material availability issues were prevalent throughout the year" ([BPC Integrated Report 2023/2024](https://www.bpc.bw/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/BPC-IR2024-2025-web.pdf)). A dated record of what stands along a route, made before the route is fixed and repeated after, is what later wayleave, compensation and community discussions go back to.

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Satellite first, drones under S.I. No. 71 of 2024" title="Drones in Botswana, and why we start from satellite"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
**Satellite and your own imagery.** A survey starts from your route file and dated satellite imagery, open building datasets or orthophotos you already hold. No aircraft flies, so satellite-based servitude and right-of-way screening needs no drone permit and is available across Botswana, however long the line.

**Drone detail.** In Botswana every drone must be registered with the Civil Aviation Authority of Botswana (CAAB), and its owners and pilots go through security vetting. Commercial work needs a CAAB RPAS Operator Certificate, which is issued only to an operator with its principal place of business in Botswana, and an authorisation for each operation, including a security review of how the camera will be used. The 2024 regulations keep drones 50 m from people, vehicles and structures outside the operation's control unless CAAB approves otherwise, and an earlier CAAB bye-law, still published on CAAB's website, also restricts flights near power lines, parks, built-up areas and major roads.

Drone surveys are subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires. Afridrone is working towards the approvals Botswana requires, and every proposal with a drone element names the company that will fly it and its CAAB approvals, so your team can check them.
