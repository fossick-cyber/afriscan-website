faq:
  - q: Is an "ROC" still the right thing to ask for in a tender?
    a: The 26th Amendment replaced the RPAS Operator Certificate (ROC) with the UAS Operator Certificate (UASOC) and its Operations Specifications on 17 March 2023, and the domestic Remote Pilot Licence (RPL) with the Remote Pilot Certificate (RPC). Many tenders and some SACAA documents still use the old names. Ask for "a UASOC (formerly ROC) with current Operations Specifications" and "an RPC (formerly RPL)" so nobody can answer the wrong question.
  - q: What is a G16 licence?
    a: G16 is a type of Class III Air Service Licence. Recent drone licences carry it with a bracketed description, such as "G16 (UAS)", but the same code also appears for manned work, for example "G16 (Flipping and Offshore)". Check that the description on the licence covers unmanned aircraft, and ask for the licence number and the gazette notice that published it.
  - q: Can a foreign company fly commercial drone surveys in South Africa?
    a: Not on its own. An Air Service Licence is granted to a South African resident or to a company incorporated in South Africa with at least 75 per cent of its voting rights held by South African residents, actively in control, flying South African aircraft, and those conditions apply at all times (Air Services Licensing Act, ss16(4) and 19). The Minister can direct an exemption from the ownership test. In practice a foreign firm works through a South African company that meets the test, or flies under one that does.
  - q: Does a satellite corridor survey need SACAA approval?
    a: No aircraft is flown, so Part 101 does not apply to the satellite work itself. POPIA still applies to personal information the imagery shows, and security measures at key points and critical infrastructure are left out of deliverables either way.
  - q: Do we need landowner permission to fly along our own servitude?
    a: SACAA's technical guidance treats landowner permission as required for every flight, signed for each operation and kept with the flight records. Whether your servitude rights let you give that permission for the strip, or the owner of the underlying land has to sign, is a question to settle with counsel before the flight plan is fixed. Agree in the contract who obtains each permission.
  - q: Can a drone fly beyond the pilot's sight along a power line or pipeline?
    a: Only if the operator's operations manual carries a B-VLOS approval under SA-CATS 101 and the pilot's RPC carries a B-VLOS rating (CAR 101.05.11 and 71.01.3). Without them, a corridor is flown in legs within the pilot's sight, up to 500 m away, or with trained observers under extended line of sight up to 1,000 m, below 400 ft.
  - q: Can a drone fly near our substation or compressor station?
    a: If the site is a national key point or a strategic installation, the operator notifies SACAA on form CA 101-20 before the flight, with the controlling authority's written permission attached, and keeps a copy on site. Ask your security team whether the site has that status; ROMPCO, for example, says its Komatipoort Compressor Station has been declared a National Key Point.

::::section{id="at-a-glance" eyebrow="At a glance" title="Four approvals, from two authorities" lead="The operator, its air service, each aircraft and each pilot are approved separately. A commercial drone job is lawful only when all four are current, and the landowner has signed for the flight."}
:::cards{cols="4"}
:::card{title="The operator" icon="building" eyebrow="SACAA"}
A **UAS Operator Certificate (UASOC)** with **Operations Specifications (OpSpec)**, required for commercial, corporate and non-profit operations ([CAR 101.04.1(1)(a)](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202303/48228rg11556gon3170.pdf)).
:::
:::card{title="The air service" icon="file-check" eyebrow="Air Service Licensing Council"}
An **Air Service Licence** for commercial operations (CAR 101.04.1(1)(b)), issued under the Air Services Licensing Act 115 of 1990, with a South African ownership and control test.
:::
:::card{title="Each aircraft" icon="drone" eyebrow="SACAA"}
A **UAS Letter of Approval (UASLA)**, valid for 12 months, and a **certificate of registration** on the South African register, with ZT- marks.
:::
:::card{title="Each pilot" icon="user-check" eyebrow="SACAA"}
A **Remote Pilot Certificate (RPC)** under Part 71, with the category and ratings the flight needs, a Part 67 medical certificate and a current currency fee.
:::
:::

Behind every one of those approvals sits the site: SACAA's technical guidance makes the landowner's signed permission a requirement for all flights ([TGM Part 101 Operations Manual](https://caasanwebsitestorage.blob.core.windows.net/flight-operations-tgm/TGM%20Part%20101%20RPAS%20Operations%20Manual.pdf), §5.5 and §6). Along a servitude that crosses many erven and farms, that permission is often the longest item in the plan.

::::section{id="terms" tone="alt" eyebrow="The names" title="Old names, new names: read tenders with both" lead="The 26th Amendment to the Civil Aviation Regulations substituted Part 101 and Part 71 on 17 March 2023 and renamed the approvals. Tenders and some SACAA documents still use the 2015 names."}
| Before 17 March 2023 | Since 17 March 2023 |
|---|---|
| RPAS, RPA | UAS, UA |
| ROC, RPAS Operator Certificate | UASOC, UAS Operator Certificate, with Operations Specifications (OpSpec) |
| RLA, RPAS Letter of Approval | UASLA, UAS Letter of Approval |
| RPL, Remote Pilot Licence (domestic) | RPC, Remote Pilot Certificate (Part 71) |
| RMT, RPAS maintenance technician | UASMT |

SACAA's own documents still mix the two sets: the fee schedule mentions "RMT authorisation", and the operations-manual guidance lists an "RLA" among the on-site documents ([26th Amendment, GN R.3170, GG 48228](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202303/48228rg11556gon3170.pdf)). "RPL" now means something else: the Part 72 Remote Pilot Licence, needed only for international flights under instrument flight rules of unmanned aircraft over 25 kg and under 150 kg. In a tender, write "UASOC (formerly ROC)" and "RPC (formerly RPL)" so nobody answers the wrong question.

::::section{id="operator" eyebrow="The operator" title="The UASOC and its Operations Specifications"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
**What goes into the application** (CAR 101.04.2): the fee, a copy of the certificate of registration for each aircraft, a UASLA for each system, and an operations manual. An aircraft may be registered under one UASOC only.

**How long it lasts, since 21 August 2026.** The [33rd Amendment](https://caasanwebsitestorage.blob.core.windows.net/legal-documents/Promulgated%2033RD%20Amendment%20Regulation%202026%20(Final).pdf) replaced the 12-month certificate with one whose period the Director of Civil Aviation sets, up to five years (101.04.3). The Operations Specifications are renewed each year with a currency fee, due 60 days before they expire; the UASOC renewal is due 90 days before the certificate expires. Renewals are issued at least 15 days before expiry, and a temporary approval is possible where one is delayed.

**What keeps it valid.** The holder must resolve audit findings and keep meeting the conditions of issue; otherwise it can no longer exercise its privileges, the Director notifies suspension, and the certificate and OpSpec are surrendered. The certificate cannot be transferred, and operations are allowed only under a *current* UASOC and OpSpec. So a certificate in a tender file is only half the answer: the OpSpec expiry date matters as much.

**What the OpSpec covers.** The specific approvals an operation relies on sit in the OpSpec and the operations manual: beyond visual line of sight, extended line of sight, controlled airspace, flights near people, structures and roads, and night flying. Ask for the pages that cover your job.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="note" title="Duties that come with the certificate"}
- **Security** (101.04.8): background checks, criminal-record checks every 24 months, secure storage, a security coordinator and Part 109 security-awareness training.
- **Records** kept for at least five years (101.04.6), and a safety management system (101.04.7).
- **Subcontractors** can be audited by SACAA (101.04.9(2)).
- **Insurance**: third-party liability cover "at all times" (101.04.12).
- **Occurrences** reported through ECCAIRS ([General Notice FOD-2023/001](https://caasanwebsitestorage.blob.core.windows.net/flight-operations-document/Unmanned%20Aerial%20System%20Operator%20Certificate%20Holders%20with%20UAS%20Occurences.pdf)).
:::
::::
:::::

SACAA publishes the list of operators with a UASOC on its [flight operations page](https://www.caa.co.za/industry-information/flight-operations/). Utilities, mining houses, metros and engineering consultancies appear on it: many large asset owners fly their own drones.
::::

::::section{id="air-service-licence" tone="alt" eyebrow="The air service" title="The Air Service Licence, and the ownership test behind it"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
Commercial operations also need an Air Service Licence (CAR 101.04.1(1)(b)) from the Air Service Licensing Council, under the Department of Transport. Recent drone licences are Class III. The Department's [Notice 3919 of 2026](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202605/54627gen3919.pdf) shows the pattern: new licences with the type "G16 (UAS)", often alongside G3 or G4, in Category A4 or H1. G16 also appears for manned work, so read the bracketed description.

The [Air Services Licensing Act 115 of 1990](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201503/act-115-1990.pdf), as enacted, sets the conditions for a licence (s16(4)):

- the service will be safe and reliable;
- the prescribed insurance is in place;
- the applicant is a South African resident or, for a company, incorporated in South Africa with **at least 75 per cent of the voting rights held by South African residents**;
- that person is **actively in control** of the service;
- the aircraft is a South African aircraft.

The licensee must meet those conditions **at all times** and hold a valid operating certificate (s19(a)–(b)). The council must exempt an applicant from the ownership test where the Minister directs (s16(5)), and may exempt it from the aircraft test (s16(6)). Applications are published in the *Gazette* for representations within 21 days (s15(1)(b)). A licence is valid indefinitely; a temporary licence lasts at most 30 days (s17).
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="legal" title="For foreign and international buyers"}
The ownership and control test means a company controlled from outside South Africa cannot fly commercial drone work there in its own name without a ministerial exemption. It works through a South African company that meets the test, or flies under one. When a proposal includes drone work, ask which company will fly, and check its licence details and ownership against the Act.

::::section{id="aircraft" eyebrow="Each aircraft" title="Letter of Approval, registration and maintenance"}
:::cards{cols="3" style="plain"}
:::card{title="UAS Letter of Approval" icon="file-check"}
No system may be operated without a UASLA, formerly the RLA. It is valid for 12 months, needs design-standard, equivalent or SA-CATS 101 system-safety documentation (101.02.1–2), and is renewed on [form CA 101-17](https://caasanwebsitestorage.blob.core.windows.net/airworthiness-forms/CA%20101-17.pdf).
:::
:::card{title="Certificate of registration" icon="clipboard"}
Each aircraft goes on the South African civil aircraft register, carries ZT- marks and is deemed South African. A change of ownership must be notified within 30 days (101.02.4).
:::
:::card{title="Maintenance" icon="shield"}
An approved maintenance programme (101.06.1). Class 3 and larger aircraft are maintained by a UASMT holder; for Class 2 and smaller, the UASOC holder may do the work if it can show it is competent (101.06.2).
:::
:::

A UASMT applicant must be 18 or over and a South African citizen, a permanent resident, or a work-permit holder with a letter of employment; the authorisation is valid for 24 months (101.06.3). SACAA's guidance also lists ICASA type-approval certificates and an ICASA radio licence among the records each aircraft needs (TGM §2.15).
::::

::::section{id="pilots" tone="alt" eyebrow="Each pilot" title="The Remote Pilot Certificate, as rewritten in August 2026"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Part 71 sets out the RPC in three categories, aeroplane, helicopter and multirotor (RPC (A), (H) and (MR)), with ratings for visual line of sight (VLOS), extended line of sight (E-VLOS) and beyond visual line of sight (B-VLOS) (71.01.3). Since the 33rd Amendment, an applicant must (71.02.1(2)):

- be 18 or over;
- have a medical certificate issued in terms of Part 67 (the earlier split between a class 3 medical and a self-declaration was deleted);
- have completed flight training at an approved training organisation (the separate radiotelephony certificate was dropped);
- have passed the theory exam within 90 days before the skills test;
- have passed a skills test run by a designated examiner under an approved training organisation, within 60 days of finishing training;
- have paid the fee.
::::
::::col
**Validity.** An RPC runs to the last day of the 24th month, as long as the annual currency fee is paid and competency is kept up (71.02.5, 71.02.7). Pilots keep logbooks for 60 months after the certificate lapses.

**Paid flying.** An RPC holder may act as pilot-in-command only if employed by a UASOC holder, flying privately under 101.01.2, or not paid for piloting (71.02.6(2)). A freelance pilot paid by the job, with no UASOC operator behind them, does not fit.

**Private flying** is limited to reduced line of sight with Class 1A or 1B aircraft, no dangerous goods and no commercial outcome (101.01.2, as substituted in August 2026). A survey for a client is never private flying.

::::section{id="rules" eyebrow="The flight" title="Operating rules that shape a servitude or settlement survey" lead="Part 101 as substituted on 17 March 2023, unless stated. Each rule below has an approval route for a UASOC holder; none of them can be waived by the client."}
### Default limits

Unless the Director authorises otherwise, a drone may not fly above 400 ft, within 10 km of an aerodrome reference point, in restricted or prohibited airspace, or adjacent to or above a nuclear power plant, prison, police station, crime scene, court of law, national key point or strategic installation (101.05.10(3)). In controlled airspace, only a UASOC holder may fly, as its operations manual approves, in visual conditions below 400 ft (101.05.3).

### The 50 m rules, which decide most corridor plans {#fifty-metres}

| Near | Rule | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| **People** | No flight overhead or within 50 m laterally of any person (101.05.13) | A UASOC and the Director's approval, unless the people are the crew or part of the controlled operation |
| **Structures and buildings** | No flight within 50 m of a structure or building (101.05.14) | A UASOC with approval, the owner's permission, and safety measures for people on the ground |
| **Public roads** | No flight over, along or within 50 m of a public road (101.05.15) | A UASOC with approval; closure of the road for any flight over it; take-off or landing on a road also needs the local authority's approval (101.05.2) |

A servitude lined with dwellings, an informal settlement or a line beside a provincial road triggers all three. That is why a drone plan along a busy corridor takes longer to approve than one over open veld, and why satellite screening usually comes first.

### Line of sight

| Mode | What it means | Source |
|---|---|---|
| VLOS | Below 400 ft, within 500 m of the pilot | TGM |
| E‑VLOS | Below 400 ft, with a trained observer, 17 or over, within 1,000 m | TGM; 101.04.4(2) |
| R‑VLOS | Within 500 m and below the highest obstacle within 300 m | TGM |
| B‑VLOS | Approval in the operations manual under SA-CATS 101, visual conditions, below 400 ft unless approved otherwise, and a B-VLOS rating on the RPC | 101.05.11; 71.01.3 |

**Night** flights need reduced line of sight, or a UASOC holder with night approval on its OpSpec (101.05.12). Except in reduced line of sight, the pilot carries an air-band radio on the air traffic service frequency and uses the aircraft registration as call-sign (101.05.16). Flying several aircraft at once needs a UASOC and approval (101.05.16A).

### On site

The pilot carries the RPC, the UASOC and OpSpec, the certificate of registration, the UASLA and the manuals (101.05.10(1)); SACAA's guidance adds proof of third-party liability cover, the operations manual, a risk assessment, NOTAMs, a map, the flight folio and the landowner permission. A first-aid kit and fire extinguishers sit within 300 m of take-off and landing (101.05.24–25), the flight folio is kept (101.05.22), the aircraft carries a 10 per cent energy reserve (101.05.23), and no alcohol is taken in the 8 hours before duty (101.05.7).

::::section{id="landowners" tone="alt" eyebrow="The site" title="Landowner permission, for every flight"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
SACAA's operations-manual guidance describes the landowner permission form as required for all flights, signed for every operation and kept in the flight records (TGM §5.5 and §6). The same guidance lists the other permissions a plan may need:

- a flexible-use-of-airspace approval for controlled, restricted or prohibited airspace, and the air traffic service unit;
- the controlling authority of any restricted or prohibited area;
- the national key point, strategic installation, police station or court concerned;
- the municipality or disaster-management authority, where relevant;
- form CA 101-18 for public events, or CA 101-20 near sensitive sites.

Along a line that crosses dozens of erven, farms and municipal properties, these letters are a deliverable in their own right. Agree at the start who obtains them: the servitude holder usually knows the landowners and has the contact history.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="Satellite first, then drones where they add something"}
Satellite screening involves no flight and no landowner letters. It covers the whole servitude first, and the drone plan, with its permissions, is limited to the stretches where the extra detail is worth the time.

::::section{id="sensitive-sites" eyebrow="Sensitive sites" title="Key points, restricted airspace and protected areas"}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="National key points and strategic installations" icon="shield"}
Before flying adjacent to or above a national key point, strategic installation, nuclear power plant, prison, police station, crime scene or court, or in restricted or prohibited airspace, the operator submits [form CA 101-20](https://caasanwebsitestorage.blob.core.windows.net/flight-operations-forms/CA%20101-20.pdf) to SACAA's flight operations inspector, with the controlling authority's written permission and, for restricted airspace, an [ATNS CAMU](https://www.camu.co.za/camuweb/camu/fsa) flexible-use approval. A copy stays on site, and an inspector may attend. Ask whether your stations have that status: [ROMPCO](https://www.rompco.co.za/about-us/) states that its Komatipoort Compressor Station has been declared a National Key Point.
:::
:::card{title="Security measures in the picture" icon="lock"}
The [Critical Infrastructure Protection Act 8 of 2019](https://static.pmg.org.za/Critical_Infra_Protection_Act8of2019.pdf) makes it an offence to take or record a photograph, video or film of the security measures at critical infrastructure (s26(1)(b)), meaning measures not clearly visible to the public (s26(2)). Whether that section is in force for your site is a question for counsel. Either way, our deliverables leave security measures at stations and installations out of the picture.
:::
:::card{title="Protected areas" icon="tree"}
Under the [Protected Areas Act](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201409/a57-03.pdf), the airspace of a special nature reserve or world heritage site extends to 2,500 ft above its highest point; aircraft may not fly below that level over it, and landing or taking off needs permission (s47, as enacted). For national parks and other reserves, ask the managing authority before planning a flight.
:::
:::card{title="Airports and airspace" icon="map"}
The 10 km radius around an aerodrome reference point and the restricted and prohibited areas published in the aeronautical information publication (AIP ENR 5.1) are checked against the route before any drone plan is costed. A long corridor can cross several.
:::

::::section{id="changes" tone="alt" eyebrow="What changed" title="2023, and two amendments on 21 August 2026"}
:::steps{style="list"}
:::step{title="1 July 2015: the first rules"}
Part 101 was inserted by the [8th Amendment](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201505/38830rg10437gon444.pdf) (GN R.444, GG 38830), with the ROC, RLA and RPL.
:::
:::step{title="17 March 2023: new names, new structure"}
The 26th Amendment substituted Part 101 and Part 71, in force on publication, with the UASOC and OpSpec, the UASLA, the RPC and the UASMT.
:::
:::step{title="21 August 2026: certificates and pilots"}
The 33rd Amendment (GN 7844, GG 55226) moved the UASOC to a period of up to five years with annual OpSpec renewal, rewrote the RPC requirements around a Part 67 medical and a designated examiner's skills test, and tied paid piloting to employment by a UASOC holder.
:::
:::step{title="21 August 2026: enforcement"}
The [36th Amendment](https://caasanwebsitestorage.blob.core.windows.net/legal-documents/Promulgated%2036th%20Amendment%20Regulations,%202026%20-%20%2021%20Aug%202026%20(Final).pdf) (GN 7845, GG 55226) amended Part 185: it added spot fines after audits and inspections and doubled the maximum administrative penalties, on separate scales for individuals and companies that rise with repeat offences.
:::
:::step{title="1 October 2026: new fees"}
The [35th Amendment](https://caasanwebsitestorage.blob.core.windows.net/legal-documents/35th%20Amendment%20-%2055257%20-%20NationalGov%20-%2028%20Aug%202026%20ENG%20and%20AFR.pdf) (GN 7861, GG 55257) replaced the Part 187 fee schedule from 1 October 2026. Budget from SACAA's current schedule, not an older one.
:::
:::

**Still to come.** SACAA's proposed amendments with comments due by 10 October 2026 do not include Part 101 or Part 71, though they touch the medical code (SA-CATS 67) and training organisations (SA-CATS 141) ([SACAA legal notices](https://www.caa.co.za/legal-notices-2/)). The Civil Aviation Amendment Act 22 of 2021 still awaits proclamation.
::::

::::section{id="penalties" eyebrow="Enforcement" title="Penalties"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
**Criminal.** An offence under the [Civil Aviation Act 13 of 2009](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201409/32266616.pdf) carries a fine, imprisonment of up to 10 years, or both (s144(1)), and the court may also cancel, suspend or endorse a licence or disqualify its holder (s144(2)). The Director may impose administrative penalties and recover them by civil action (s144(5)–(8)).
::::
::::col
**Administrative.** Part 185, as amended by the 36th Amendment, gives the Director a ladder of sanctions: a counselling letter, a warning, remedial training, an administrative penalty, and suspension, downgrading, endorsement, revocation or cancellation (185.03.1), plus spot fines (185.03.2). Suspension or revocation is possible for safety or security reasons or obstruction, or after 14 days' notice (185.04.1), with an appeal within 30 days (185.04.2). Missed renewals and unresolved findings can suspend a UASOC (101.04.3).

::::section{id="data" tone="alt" eyebrow="Beyond aviation" title="Personal information, security features and land surveys"}
:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="POPIA" icon="lock" key="za-popia"}
Aerial imagery of identifiable people, homes and businesses can be personal information. Operator contracts, safeguards, breach notices, cross-border transfers and prior authorisation for some evidence work all apply. What that means for a survey.
:::
:::card{title="Orthophotos are not cadastral surveys" icon="ruler"}
Surveys for registrable diagrams and the fixing of registered boundaries are reserved to professional land surveyors ([Geomatics Profession Act, s13(2)(b)](https://www.sagc.org.za/pdf/legislation/Geomatics%20Profession%20Act%20-%20Act%2019%20of%202013.pdf)), and a servitude diagram needs Surveyor-General approval ([Land Survey Act 8 of 1997](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201409/a8-97.pdf)). Drone and satellite registers support that work; they do not replace it.
:::
:::card{title="Satellite work" icon="satellite"}
No aircraft is flown, so Part 101 does not apply to satellite screening. The national aerial imagery custodian is the [NGI](https://ngi.dlrrd.gov.za/); state bodies may already license imagery through SANSA's centralised procurement, which is worth checking before anything new is bought.
