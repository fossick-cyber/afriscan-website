faq:
  - q: Is satellite imagery personal information?
    a: It can be. POPIA covers information about an identifiable, living natural person and, unusually, an existing juristic person, and its list of examples includes location information. "Images" are not in that list, but imagery that lets someone identify a person, a home or a business can still contain personal information. Very-high-resolution satellite imagery shows roofs and yards, not faces; drone imagery can show far more.
  - q: Who is the responsible party for a survey of our servitude?
    a: Usually you. The responsible party is whoever decides the purpose and means of the processing; a contractor that processes the imagery on your behalf is an operator. POPIA then requires a written contract between you (s21), security safeguards (s19) and notification of any security compromise (s22). Your counsel settles the roles for your case.
  - q: Do we need prior authorisation from the Information Regulator?
    a: Possibly, if the register is used as information on unlawful or objectionable conduct on behalf of third parties, for example to support action over encroachment or unlawful occupation. Section 57(1)(b) requires the responsible party to obtain authorisation before that processing starts, and section 58 gives the Regulator up to 13 weeks where it investigates. Registers used for planning, maintenance or engagement raise a different question. Take legal advice before you decide how a register will be used.
  - q: Does AfriScan identify the people who live in the structures?
    a: No. The register lists structures with an ID, a location, a distance to the line and a date. It carries no names, and we do not tag, count or follow people or vehicles in any imagery.
  - q: Where is our imagery processed and stored?
    a: The proposal for your work says where the imagery and results will be processed and stored and for how long, so your information officer can check the section 72 basis for any transfer outside South Africa before work starts.

::::section{id="personal-information" tone="alt" eyebrow="The scope" title="When imagery holds personal information"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
The [Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201409/3706726-11act4of2013protectionofpersonalinforcorrect.pdf) covers information relating to an identifiable, living natural person and to an existing juristic person. Its list of examples is not exhaustive and includes location information and biometric information. "Images" are not named in the list, but that does not take imagery outside the Act: a picture that identifies a person, a home or a business can still be personal information.

For a servitude survey, what matters is what the imagery shows and what the register adds to it:

- **Satellite imagery** shows roofs, yards, tracks and cleared ground. It does not show faces, but a dated, located structure can still point to a household.
- **Drone orthophotos** can show people, vehicles, number plates and the inside of yards, depending on the height and camera.
- **The register** adds coordinates, a distance to the line and a date to each structure. Linked with a municipal valuation roll or a list of owners, it becomes information about identifiable people.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="note" title="What our registers contain"}
- A structure ID and coordinates
- The distance to your line and the band
- The imagery source and date
- A reviewer's category, where scoped (main building, outbuilding, under construction)

No names, no ownership, no occupancy and no people.

::::section{id="roles" eyebrow="Who carries which duty" title="Responsible party and operator"}
The **responsible party** decides the purpose and means of processing. When a line owner, utility, mine or municipality commissions a survey of its own servitudes and sites, it is usually the responsible party. A contractor that processes the imagery and produces the register on its behalf is an **operator**. The Act puts duties on both:

:::cards{cols="3" style="plain"}
:::card{title="A written contract (s21)" icon="file-text"}
The responsible party must have a written contract with its operator that requires the operator to keep the safeguards in section 19. Put it in the survey contract, not in an email.
:::
:::card{title="Security safeguards (s19)" icon="shield"}
Appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures to prevent loss, damage and unlawful access. In practice: named recipients, controlled storage and a retention period agreed in advance.
:::
:::card{title="Security compromises (s22)" icon="alert"}
Where there are reasonable grounds to believe personal information has been accessed or acquired by an unauthorised person, the responsible party notifies the Regulator and the people affected. The operator tells the responsible party at once.
:::
:::

The Act also allows administrative fines (s109) and, for some offences, imprisonment (s107). Whether a particular survey needs any more than this depends on how its results will be used.

::::section{id="prior-authorisation" tone="alt" eyebrow="Evidence work" title="Prior authorisation under section 57" lead="The provision every encroachment programme in South Africa should read before a register is used as evidence."}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
Section 57(1)(b) requires a responsible party to obtain the Information Regulator's authorisation **before** processing "information on criminal behaviour or on unlawful or objectionable conduct on behalf of third parties". Authorisation is needed once, unless the processing changes (s57(4)), and not where a code of conduct for the sector is in force (s57(3)).

Once notified, the Regulator has four weeks to decide whether to investigate, and up to 13 weeks where it does; the processing waits in the meantime (s58). Processing without notifying is an offence (s59), punishable by a fine or up to 12 months' imprisonment (s107(b)).

The Regulator's [guidance note on prior authorisation](https://inforegulator.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/InfoRegSA-GuidanceNote-PriorAuthorisation-20210311-1.pdf) gives criminal-record, reference and disciplinary checks as examples. It does not deal with aerial imagery. Whether a register of new structures on a servitude, used to support action over encroachment or unlawful occupation, falls within section 57(1)(b) has not been settled in the sources we checked. It may.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="legal" title="Before a register becomes evidence"}
Ask your counsel three things:

1. Is your organisation the responsible party for this processing?
2. Does the intended use fall under section 57(1)(b)?
3. If so, has authorisation been obtained, and does it cover this programme?

On South African work we scope registers for planning, maintenance and engagement. We don't package them as evidence about unlawful conduct unless your counsel has confirmed the POPIA position.

::::section{id="cross-border" eyebrow="Transfers" title="Imagery and results that leave South Africa"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Section 72 limits transfers of personal information to a third party in a foreign country. A transfer is allowed where the recipient is subject to a law, binding corporate rules or an agreement that gives adequate protection, where the data subject consents, or on another basis the section lists.

Imagery processed by a service outside South Africa, satellite scenes delivered from an overseas archive, and results stored abroad can all involve a transfer. The basis needs to be settled before the imagery moves, not after.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="note" title="In every proposal"}
The proposal for your work states where the imagery and results will be processed and stored, and for how long, so your information officer can check the section 72 basis before work starts. If your organisation requires processing in South Africa, say so in your request.

::::section{id="security-measures" tone="alt" eyebrow="Installations" title="Security measures stay out of the picture"}
Stations, substations and plants along a servitude may be national key points or critical infrastructure. The [Critical Infrastructure Protection Act 8 of 2019](https://static.pmg.org.za/Critical_Infra_Protection_Act8of2019.pdf) makes it an offence to take or record a photograph, video or film of the security measures at critical infrastructure (s26(1)(b)), meaning measures that are not clearly visible to the public (s26(2)); whether that section is in force for a given site is a question for counsel. Drone flights near these sites also need SACAA notice on form CA 101-20 ([drone law](/za/drone-regulations#sensitive-sites)).

Our deliverables leave the security measures at stations and installations out of every image and report, and we never publish imagery of a client's installations.

:::::columns{split="1-1"}

### What we do

:::checklist
- Map structures, cleared ground, excavations and tracks, not people
- Keep names, ownership and occupancy out of the register
- Send results only to the contacts you name
- State in the proposal where imagery and results are processed and stored, and for how long
- Work under the operator terms in your contract
- Leave security measures at installations out of deliverables
:::
::::
::::col
### What we don't do

:::checklist{tone="no"}
- Identify, tag, count or follow people or vehicles
- Publish your routes, imagery or results without written permission
- Link structures to named owners or occupiers
- Package registers as evidence about unlawful conduct before your counsel has confirmed the POPIA position
