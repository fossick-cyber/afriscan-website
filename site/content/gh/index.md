faq:
  - q: Which widths do you measure in Ghana?
    a: "The ones that apply to your asset. For 161 kV and 330 kV transmission lines we report the protected strips of 15 m and 20 m either side of the centre of the line, as GRIDCo describes the transmission-line protection regulations. For pipelines we measure from the centreline you send, or from the corridor boundary if you send it as a polygon, to the width in your acquisition instrument, wayleave or company standard. For a mining lease we work from the boundary you give us, with a ring around it. Wider reporting bands, such as 50, 100 and 200 m, can be added to any of these."
  - q: Do you need anyone on site to start?
    a: No. A survey starts from your route or boundary file and dated satellite imagery, open building datasets or imagery you already hold, so nobody travels and no drone flies. That matters where access is difficult or unsafe. Drone detail is added only where a stretch needs it, subject to the GCAA authorisations and security clearances each job requires.
  - q: Does this replace right-of-way patrols or lease inspections?
    a: No. It tells your patrol, land and community teams where the ground has changed since the last survey, so they go there first, and it gives them a dated record to take with them.
  - q: Can you tell us who built a structure or who is digging?
    a: No. We map the land, not people. The register records a structure, a pit or a patch of cleared ground, where it is and on which imagery date it first appears. It never identifies, counts or follows anyone, and whether a structure or a working is authorised is for you and the authorities to decide.
  - q: Can the register be shared with chiefs, the District Assembly or the people along the line?
    a: That is your decision. A neutral, dated map of what stood where is often the starting point for engagement with traditional authorities and communities, for compensation processes and for grievance handling. We never publish maps of your assets or of the settlements around them without your written permission.
  - q: Do you work with Ghanaian firms?
    a: Yes. On mining leases, the Minerals Commission's procurement list reserves mapping for mines to firms incorporated in Ghana with Ghanaian owners, so AfriScan works there as the technology supplier to a qualifying Ghanaian firm, which holds the contract. ::::section{id="law" eyebrow="Already in Ghana's law" title="Lines on the map that only mean something if you can see the ground" lead="Ghanaian law draws protected strips along power lines, needs the lease holder's consent for new buildings in a mining area and fixes compensation to a cut-off date. Each of those rules depends on a record of what stood where, and when."}
:::cards{cols="3"}
:::card{title="Power lines: protected strips either side" icon="power" eyebrow="L.I. 542, as amended by L.I. 1737"}
The transmission-line protection regulations bar drilling, excavation, lorry parks, shops, garages, barns and real-estate development within 15 m either side of the centre of a 161 kV line and 20 m of a 330 kV line, as GRIDCo described them in September 2026. You can only plan for what you can see on the ground.
:::
:::card{title="Mining areas: building needs consent" icon="mine" eyebrow="Act 703, s.72(4)"}
Under the [Minerals and Mining Act](https://www.mincom.gov.gh/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Minerals-and-Mining-Act-2006-Act-703.pdf), the owner or lawful occupier of land in a mining area "shall not erect a building or a structure without the consent of the holder of the mining lease". A dated record shows what stood there, and from when.
:::
:::card{title="Compensation: fixed to a cut-off date" icon="calendar" eyebrow="L.I. 2175, regs 1(8) and 16"}
Ghana's [mining compensation regulations](https://www.mincom.gov.gh/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Minerals-and-Mining-Compensation-Resettlement-Regulations-2012-L.I.-2175.pdf) use a cut-off date published in the district, and treat "speculative development" after it differently. A structure inventory on that date supports fair, transparent compensation.
:::
:::

::::section{id="problem" tone="alt" eyebrow="Why it matters" title="What changes between visits" lead="Ghana's pipelines, transmission lines and mining leases sit among farms, towns, rivers and forest reserves. The ground beside them changes faster than any inspection cycle can write down."}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="“New structures keep appearing under the line.”" icon="houses"}
Kiosks, sheds, workshops and houses go up inside protected strips, most often where a line runs beside a road or a town grows towards it. Each one is easier to plan for, and to discuss, while it is new and few.
:::
:::card{title="“The ground has been dug up near our boundary.”" icon="excavation"}
Galamsey pits, washing sites, spoil and cleared ground can open up inside a lease, at its edge or close to a buried pipeline or cable. Imagery shows where and when the land changed; your team decides what happens next.
:::
:::card{title="“We need a record before the new route is fixed.”" icon="route"}
A new pipeline, line or road starts with route options and an environmental permit application. A dated count of the structures along each option, taken before the investment decision, is the record later discussions go back to.
:::
:::card{title="“The cut-off date is coming.”" icon="calendar"}
When a mining lease expands, a new line is acquired or a resettlement plan starts, the structures standing on the cut-off date decide who is compensated for what. The census teams need to know where to go.
:::

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Satellite first, drones where the approvals allow" title="Two routes to the picture, and the rules for each"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
**Satellite and your own imagery.** A survey starts from your route or lease file and dated satellite imagery, open building datasets or the orthophotos your own drone teams already fly. Satellite-based screening needs no drone permit, because nothing flies, and it covers a whole corridor before anyone decides where a team should go.

**Drone detail.** Where a stretch needs more detail than satellite imagery shows, a drone survey captures it, subject to the GCAA authorisations and security clearances each job requires. In Ghana, commercial drone work needs an RPAS Operator Certificate that only a company registered in Ghana can obtain, registered drones with permits, and pilots with remote pilot licences. The GCAA and the Ministry of National Security have set no-fly zones around security facilities, airports, power stations and ports, flights within 30 m of buildings, vehicles or groups of people need explicit permission, and work near high-tension wires needs a special authorisation. Afridrone is working towards the authorisations Ghana requires, and every drone proposal lists the approvals its flights need.

::::section{id="working-in-ghana" tone="alt" eyebrow="For procurement and compliance teams" title="Local content, supplier registration and data protection"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Mining contracts in Ghana follow the Minerals Commission's Local Procurement List, which reserves ground-based survey and mapping for mines to firms incorporated in Ghana with exclusively Ghanaian directors and shareholders. Upstream petroleum service companies need a Petroleum Commission permit, public buyers procure through GHANEPS, and organisations that process personal data register with the Data Protection Commission. Our notes set out what each rule means for mapping and drone work, and what to ask any supplier for.
