faq:
  - q: Which widths do you measure in Angola?
    a: The 30 m strip on each side that the Land Law attaches to electricity, water, telecom, oil and gas installations and conductors (Lei n.º 9/04, art. 27(7)(g)), and any width you give us, such as the one in your concession contract, a constituted servitude or a company standard, for example 60 m and 100 m. In a mining concession we measure the zones from the polygons you send. One survey can carry up to six widths.
  - q: Does a satellite-only survey need ANAC authorisation?
    a: The Civil Aviation Law's authorisations concern aircraft, and no aircraft flies in satellite work. That is a reading of the text, not legal advice. If the deliverables are meant as official cartography or geographic information, check with IGCA whether an alvará or homologation applies (Decreto Presidencial n.º 115/21, art. 6). See [drone law in Angola](/ao/drone-regulations).
  - q: Can the report be in Portuguese?
    a: Yes. The PDF report comes in Portuguese or English, so the same register can go to the land team in Luanda, a provincial government and an international lender. The GIS layers open in QGIS, ArcGIS and Google Earth.
  - q: Do you work in every province?
    a: We do no field or drone work in Cabinda province outside Cabinda city, or in Lunda Norte and Lunda Sul, in line with current travel advice. Elsewhere, any field or drone work starts with a landmine and unexploded-ordnance risk assessment agreed with the project.
  - q: Is the register a topographic or cadastral survey?
    a: No. It is an inventory of structures and land change made from imagery, for managing servitudes and concessions. Official cartography and the property cadastre belong to IGCA, which grants the alvarás for that work and homologates geographic information (Decreto Presidencial n.º 115/21, art. 6).
  - q: Does the register hold personal data?
    a: It describes structures and land, with no names, ID numbers or photographs of people. Household lists, where a project keeps them, stay with you. Lei n.º 22/11 makes processing personal data subject to prior notification to the data-protection agency (APD) or its authorisation (art. 35), and a transfer to a country without adequate protection always needs APD authorisation (art. 34).
  - q: Will you publish our route or results?
    a: No. We never publish a client's route, imagery or results without written permission. The sample on this site, a high-pressure gas pipeline in Mozambique, is shown with the route owner's permission.

::::section{id="law" eyebrow="The 30 m strip" title="What Angola's Land Law says about land beside pipes and lines" lead="Angola's Land Law places a 30 m strip on each side of electricity, water, telecom, oil and gas installations and conductors among its partial reserves. Our register measures each structure against that strip and against the widths you add."}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
- **[Lei n.º 9/04, de 9 de Novembro (Lei de Terras)](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/ang49570.pdf), art. 27(7)(g):** partial reserves include "os terrenos ocupados por auto-estradas, por estradas de quatro faixas e por instalações e condutores de electricidade, água, telecomunicações, petróleo e gás com uma faixa confinante de 30m de cada lado" (land occupied by motorways, four-lane roads and electricity, water, telecom, oil and gas installations and conductors, with an adjoining strip of 30 m on each side).
- **What the strip allows (n.º 6):** every form of occupation or use that does not collide with the purposes set in the reserve's constituting instrument.
- **Land the State does not own (n.º 9–10):** it enters a reserve only by expropriation for public utility or by administrative servitude, and fair compensation is always due.
- **Who creates reserves (n.º 2):** the Government. Whether the 30 m strip binds a given stretch without its own constituting act is a question for the project's counsel; the register shows what stands inside it either way.
- **Before petroleum operations:** [Decreto n.º 120/08](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/ang119457.pdf) requires the area's social and population structure and its land-rights holders to be assessed before a prospecting licence, and the assessment to be updated before exploration and again before development and production, including along pipeline routes and "servidões legais de passagem" (art. 11).

::::col

::::section{id="problem" tone="alt" eyebrow="Why it matters" title="What builds up between two visits" lead="A pipeline, a transmission line or a concession covers more ground than any team can walk. Occupation of a servitude rarely starts as a whole bairro: it starts with one house, then the one beside it."}
Between patrols, houses and outbuildings go up beside the strip, fields (lavras) and tracks open, and someone digs or clears ground near the asset. By the time anyone notices, what could have been handled early has become a servitude negotiation, a line in a resettlement plan or an argument about who was there first.

In Angola the timing matters in law as well as on the ground. In expropriation by zones or alignments, compensation for property outside the first zone counts the necessary improvements (*benfeitorias necessárias*) made between the declaration of public utility and taking possession ([Lei n.º 1/21, art. 33](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/ang201252.pdf)). A dated register on each of those dates separates what existed from what came later.

::::section{id="resettlement" eyebrow="Resettlement and ESIA" title="Dated registers for cut-off dates, expropriation and regrouping"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
**Cut-off dates.** On lender-financed projects the lender applies standards such as IFC Performance Standard 5 or the World Bank's Environmental and Social Standard 5, and the census cut-off date decides who is eligible for compensation. A register made from dated imagery supports the cut-off-date record and shows what appeared afterwards. It does not replace the census, the asset inventory or consultation with the families concerned.

**Expropriation by zones.** Lei n.º 1/21 leaves forced removal and resettlement operations to their own instrument (art. 2(2)–(3)), and counts improvements made between the declaration of public utility and possession in the compensation for later zones (art. 33).
::::
::::col
**Mining zones and regrouping.** The Mining Code bars residence in restricted zones and allows the zones to be created only if the residents are regrouped nearby, in housing "nunca inferiores" (never inferior) to what they had, with equivalent social infrastructure (Lei n.º 31/11, art. 207). A dated count of structures before and after is the kind of baseline such a programme starts from.

**Environmental licensing.** The impact-assessment procedure is set by [Decreto Presidencial n.º 117/20](https://anrm.gov.ao/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/DP-117-20-de-22-de-Abril-Novo-Regulamento-Geral-de-Avaliacao-de-Impacte-Ambiental-e-do-Procedimento-de-Licenciamento-Ambiental.pdf). Our layers drop into the ESIA (EIAS) team's maps.

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Satellite first, drones with ANAC authorisation" title="Two routes to the picture, and the rules for each"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
Satellite screening starts from the route file and dated imagery, open building databases or imagery you already hold. No aircraft flies and nobody needs to go on site, however long the strip. AfriScan is Afridrone's land and corridor monitoring service; Afridrone flies the drone work.

Where satellite imagery is not enough, we propose drone surveys of the flagged stretches, subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires. In Angola, no aircraft able to fly without a pilot may overfly the territory without a special authorisation from the Autoridade Nacional da Aviação Civil (Lei da Aviação Civil, art. 16.º-A), and aerial photography, photogrammetry and aerial topography are specialised aerial services whose requirements ANAC sets (arts. 54–55).

Afridrone is working towards the operator approvals each country requires. Every drone proposal says who flies, under which authorisations and on what timetable.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="legal" title="Who authorises what, in short"}
- **ANAC:** the special authorisation for each flight, aerial-work licensing and aircraft registration (Lei n.º 28/21, art. 37; Lei da Aviação Civil, arts. 16.º-A and 156).
- **IGCA:** alvarás for cartography, topography and GIS, and homologation of geographic information (Decreto Presidencial n.º 115/21, art. 6).
- **Mining-right holders and concessionaires:** access to the restricted and protection zones of mines (Código Mineiro, arts. 200–203) and to line servitudes.
- **APD:** processing and transfer of personal data (Lei n.º 22/11).

We do no field or drone work in Cabinda province outside Cabinda city, or in the provinces of Lunda Norte and Lunda Sul. That follows current travel advice: the UK advises against all but essential travel to Cabinda province except Cabinda city, and against travel to Lunda Norte and Lunda Sul unless absolutely necessary ([FCDO, updated 28 May 2026](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/angola)); Canada advises avoiding non-essential travel to Cabinda and Lunda Norte ([Travel Canada, 24 September 2026](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/angola)).

The same UK advice notes mines and unexploded weapons in many parts of the country. In the other provinces, any field work or flight therefore starts with an assessment of that risk agreed with the project, and keeps to the routes the project sets.
