faq:
  - q: Which widths do you measure in Namibia?
    a: The ones that apply to your asset. Namibian power-line servitude widths are set in each wayleave agreement, road reserves are set by the Roads Ordinance, and the Minerals and Petroleum Acts set 100 m and 300 m consent distances around structures and surveyed erven. Send the widths with the route, or the servitude and licence polygons themselves, and we report each structure's distance to the line or boundary and the band it falls in, with up to six widths in one survey.
  - q: Does a satellite survey need NCAA approval or landowner permission?
    a: No aircraft is flown, so the NCAA's Part 101 approvals and the landowner permission a drone flight needs do not apply to the satellite work itself; counsel should confirm this for your project. Drone stretches, where you want them, are planned separately and are subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires.
  - q: Can you work in diamond restricted areas and national parks?
    a: From satellite imagery, yes, because nobody enters the area. Entering or being in a diamond restricted area needs a Minister's permit under the Diamond Act, and whether a drone overflight also needs one is unresolved, so the licence holder's consent and counsel's view come first. In a national park a drone flight needs approval, and filming needs MEFT approval. See [drone law in Namibia](/na/drone-regulations#restricted-areas).
  - q: Can you tell us whether a structure is lawful?
    a: No. We map what stands where and when it first appears in the imagery. Whether a structure is covered by a wayleave, a customary land right, a lease or a permission under the roads ordinances is for your land team, the land board, the traditional authority, the Roads Authority or the courts to decide. The register gives them one dated, located list to work from.
  - q: Do you detect copper theft or vandalism?
    a: No. Satellites cannot see theft, vandalism or people. We map land change, such as new structures, fresh digging, cleared ground and new tracks along your lines and routes, so your field teams know where to look first.
  - q: Is the register a survey we can register at the Surveyor-General?
    a: No. General plans and diagrams need approval and must be signed by a land surveyor under the Land Survey Act 1993. Our registers are monitoring and planning records built from imagery. They show your surveyor and your wayleave officers where to look, and they support your land, compensation and community processes.

::::section{id="law" eyebrow="Already in your agreements and the law" title="The distances your register is measured against" lead="Namibian wayleave agreements, road ordinances and licence laws already draw lines around your assets. A line only protects the asset if someone can see what stands inside it, and when it arrived."}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="Power lines: widths by wayleave, clearances by the Code" icon="power" eyebrow="Wayleaves · Electricity Safety Code"}
Power-line servitude widths are set in wayleave agreements. For one new 400 kV line, NamPower's published resettlement framework describes an 80 m servitude in which "no permanent structures other than the towers are allowed", with a 12 m strip cleared of vegetation ([NamPower, November 2023](https://www.nampower.com.na/Media/Document/2029f04a-3d58-4a66-8302-31ea9d6ef0c7.pdf)). The [Electricity Safety Code](https://www.ecb.org.na/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Namibia-Electricity-Safety-Code.pdf) sets minimum clearances between live conductors and structures, from 3.0 m up to 33 kV to 5.6 m at 400 kV (Table 1).
:::
:::card{title="Roads: a 60 m reserve and a 100 m building restriction" icon="route" eyebrow="Roads Ordinance 1972 · Ribbon Development Ordinance 1960"}
Trunk, main and district roads have a 60 m road reserve, 30 m either side of the centre line where the boundaries have not been set otherwise, and farm roads 30 m ([Roads Ordinance, s.3](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Roads%20Ordinance%2017%20of%201972.pdf)). Along trunk, main and building restriction roads, no structure may be put up within 100 m of the centre line without written permission, with exceptions for urban areas, existing township erven and low fences ([Ribbon Development Ordinance, s.8](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Advertising%20on%20Roads%20and%20Ribbon%20Development%20Ordinance%2030%20of%201960.pdf)).
:::
:::card{title="Mining and petroleum: 100 m and 300 m" icon="mine" eyebrow="Minerals Act 1992 s.52 · Petroleum Act 1991 s.16"}
Licence holders need the owner's prior written permission before working within 100 m of "any spring, well, borehole, reservoir, dam, dipping-tank… kraal, building or any structure of whatever nature", or within 300 m of a surveyed erf ([Minerals Act, s.52](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Minerals%20(Prospecting%20and%20Mining)%20Act%2033%20of%201992.pdf); [Petroleum Act, s.16](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Petroleum%20(Exploration%20and%20Production)%20Act%202%20of%201991.pdf)). A dated register of what stands near the planned workings is where that conversation starts.
:::
:::card{title="Communal land: rights, fences and land boards" icon="boundary" eyebrow="Communal Land Reform Act 2002"}
On communal land, nobody may occupy or use land except under a right acquired under the Act (s.43), fences need authorisation (ss.18, 44), and a land board can grant occupational land rights for government and state-owned-enterprise projects (s.36A) ([Communal Land Reform Act](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Communal%20Land%20Reform%20Act%205%20of%202002.pdf)). Chiefs, traditional authorities and land boards decide; we give them and you one dated map of what stands on the land.
:::
:::

The [Roads Act 4 of 2025](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Roads%20Act%204%20of%202025.pdf), not yet in force, would keep the reserve widths and add a building restriction of 45 m from the centre line in township areas and 500 m around intersections with other roads and railways (s.38(5)). Until it commences, the 1960 and 1972 Ordinances apply.

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Satellite first, drones under Part 101" title="Two routes to the picture, and the rules for each"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
**Satellite and your own imagery.** A survey starts from your route or boundary file and dated satellite imagery, open building datasets or the orthophotos you already hold. No aircraft flies and nobody needs to enter the land, so diamond areas, national parks and remote farms are covered the same way as the rest of the route.

**Drone detail.** Where a stretch needs more detail than satellite imagery shows, a drone survey captures it, subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires. In Namibia that means an NCAA RPAS Letter of Approval under NAMCAR Part 101 for each operation, applied for at least 30 days ahead, pilots with NCAA remote pilot certificates, and an RPAS Operator Certificate for any flight beyond visual line of sight, which the NCAA issues only to Namibian persons and companies. Flights over private property need the owner's permission, and parks, diamond areas and restricted airspace need their own.

Afridrone is working towards the approvals Namibia requires. Every drone proposal names the company that will fly and its approvals, so your team can check them.
