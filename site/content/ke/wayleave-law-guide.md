The Land Act 2012 sets how it is created, who must be told, what is compensated and where disputes go, and the energy, petroleum, roads and mining laws add their own rules. This guide sets out the procedure and the sections behind each step for wayleave, land and RAP teams and their counsel, as last reviewed on 27 September 2026. It is not legal advice.

faq:
  - q: Is the Wayleaves Act still in force?
    a: No. The Land Act 2012 repealed the Wayleaves Act (Cap. 292) and the Land Acquisition Act (Cap. 295) in its Schedule (s.161). Some published utility pages still cite the Wayleaves Act, so check which law a document relies on.
  - q: Who serves the notice, the National Land Commission or the applicant?
    a: The applicant. Section 144(4) requires the body applying for the wayleave to serve notice on all occupiers of the land, including holders of customary pastoral rights, on the county, and on everyone in actual occupation of urban and peri-urban land along the route. The Commission then reviews the application.
  - q: When does a wayleave take effect?
    a: Thirty days after the Cabinet Secretary's Gazette order is published (s.146). The order delineates the route, is notified to the county and publicised, and can be appealed on a point of law within six weeks. The Registrar then records the route on the titles it crosses (s.147).
  - q: Does a wayleave bind people who hold no title?
    a: Yes. It attaches to the servient land and binds "all owners from time to time" of it, including those who hold under customary law and those who occupy it in any other way (s.143(3)). The notice before it goes to occupiers as well as owners (s.144(4)).
  - q: Can a county charge for a wayleave for energy infrastructure?
    a: The Energy Act 2019 says no public body may charge levies on public energy infrastructure without the Cabinet Secretary's written consent (s.223). The Energy (Amendment) Bill 2025 would exempt county governments from needing that consent (Capital FM, 6 October 2025); check its status before relying on either position.
  - q: What if a landowner cannot be found before a survey?
    a: For energy infrastructure, entry to survey needs the owner's prior consent. Where the owner cannot be traced, the Energy Act requires 15 days' notice through at least two national newspapers and on local radio for two weeks (s.171).
  - q: Where do disputes about compensation go?
    a: To the Land Acquisition Tribunal in the first instance, for compulsory acquisition and wayleave matters, "despite the provisions of sections 127, 128 and 148(5)" (s.133C(6)), and then to the Environment and Land Court.

::::section{id="in-short" eyebrow="In short" title="Four things the Land Act settles"}
:::cards{cols="4"}
:::card{title="What a wayleave is" icon="corridor" eyebrow="s.143"}
A right for national or county government, a public authority or a corporate body that "shall attach to and run with the servient land", binding every owner and occupier from time to time.
:::
:::card{title="Who is told" icon="houses" eyebrow="s.144(4)"}
Every occupier, including holders of customary pastoral rights, the county, and everyone in actual occupation of urban and peri-urban land, served by the applicant.
:::
:::card{title="How it is made" icon="file-text" eyebrow="s.146"}
By Gazette order of the Cabinet Secretary after the National Land Commission's review, in force 30 days after publication.
:::
:::card{title="What is compensated" icon="scale" eyebrow="ss.2 and 148"}
The use of the land and damage to trees, crops and buildings, including damage from preliminary survey work, valued as at the notice of intention.
:::

::::section{id="procedure" tone="alt" eyebrow="The procedure" title="From application to title register, step by step" lead="Land Act 2012, as consolidated to the Land (Amendment) Act 2025, in force on 4 November 2025: the sections on wayleaves and public rights of way."}
:::steps{style="list"}
:::step{title="The body that needs the wayleave applies"}
A wayleave may be created for national or county government, a public authority "or any corporate body" (s.143). The Commission may create a public right of way "subject to… section 146".
:::
:::step{title="The applicant serves notice"}
On all occupiers of the land, including those with "customary pastoral rights"; on the county; on "all persons in actual occupation of land in an urban and per-urban area"; and on other interested persons (s.144(4)). This is the step where a map of the structures along the route shows where the notice has to reach.
:::
:::step{title="The National Land Commission reviews and recommends"}
After at least 30 days, the Commission recommends one of three routes to the Cabinet Secretary: a public inquiry, a referral to the county, or negotiations (s.146).
:::
:::step{title="The Cabinet Secretary makes a Gazette order"}
The order delineates the route. It is notified to the county and publicised, comes into force 30 days after publication, and can be appealed on a point of law within six weeks (s.146).
:::
:::step{title="The Registrar records the route"}
On the titles of the land it crosses (s.147). The wayleave then binds every owner from time to time, under customary law or however they occupy the land (s.143(3)), and the holder's staff, agents and contractors may enter to build, maintain and pass.
:::
:::step{title="The applicant pays compensation"}
For the use of the land and for damage to "trees crops and buildings" (s.148(1)), including damage from "any preliminary work undertaken in connection with surveying or determining the route" (s.148(3)), paid promptly by the applicant (s.148(4)). Disputes go first to the Land Acquisition Tribunal (s.133C(6)).
:::
:::

The Land Act's Schedule (s.161) repealed the **Wayleaves Act (Cap. 292)** and the **Land Acquisition Act (Cap. 295)**. Some published utility pages still cite the Wayleaves Act.

::::section{id="definitions" class="compare" eyebrow="Words that decide cases" title="Four definitions in section 2"}
| Term | What the Act says | Why it matters for a register |
|---|---|---|
| **Building** | "any structure or erection of any kind whatsoever whether permanent or temporary, whether movable or immovable and whether completed or uncompleted" | Kiosks, stalls, mabati sheds and unfinished structures all count |
| **Full** (compensation) | For compulsory acquisition "or creation of wayleaves, easements and public rights of way", "the restoration of the value of the land, including improvements thereon, as at the date of the notice of intention to acquire the land" | The notice date is the valuation date |
| **Prompt** | "within a reasonable time of, and in any case not more than one year after, the taking of possession" | Payments run on a clock from possession |
| **Public purposes** | Include transportation, such as roads and railways; public utilities for "electricity, gas, communication"; and security and defence installations | Pipelines, power lines, roads, railways and fibre can all qualify |

::::section{id="acquisition" tone="alt" eyebrow="Compulsory acquisition" title="When land is acquired, not just crossed" lead="Part VIII of the Land Act (sections 107–133), run by the National Land Commission."}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
- **Geo-referencing.** "All land to be compulsorily acquired shall be geo-referenced and authenticated" by the survey authority (s.107(8)).
- **Occupants without title.** Occupants in good faith of land compulsorily acquired who may not hold title are compensated on (a) actual occupation for "an uninterrupted period of six years immediately before the publication of the notice of intention" and (b) "improvements done before the date of publication in the Gazette of the notice" (s.107A(9)).
- **Lapse.** An acquisition "not completed within twenty-four months from the date of publication of the notice of intention" lapses (s.111(1B)).
- **No one left homeless.** Taking possession must "not result in persons being rendered homeless" (s.120(1)).
- **Disputes.** The Land Acquisition Tribunal first (s.133C(6)), then the Environment and Land Court.

**Evictions from public land** follow their own procedure in sections 152A to 152I, added by the Land Laws (Amendment) Act 2016. The Commission gives notice in writing, in the Gazette, in one national newspaper and by radio in a local language "at least three months before the eviction" (s.152C); for community land, a similar notice goes through the county (s.152D).

::::section{id="sector-laws" class="compare" eyebrow="Sector laws" title="What the energy, petroleum, roads, mining and community land laws add"}
| Law | Section | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Energy Act 2019 | s.170 | Energy infrastructure may be developed "on, through, over or under any public, community or private land" |
| | s.171 | The owner's prior consent for entry to survey; if the owner cannot be traced, 15 days' notice through at least two national newspapers and local radio for two weeks |
| | s.173(1)(b) | Occupiers claim for loss or damage within three months after the development |
| | s.176 | Entry to operate, inspect, repair or remove |
| | s.178 | Lines and pipelines across streets, roads, railways, forests, national parks and reserves |
| | s.223 | No public body may charge levies on public energy infrastructure without the Cabinet Secretary's written consent |
| Kenya Roads Act 2007 | s.49(1) | No structure or other thing on, over or below a road reserve or in a building restricted area without the road authority's written permission |
| | s.27 | Pipe-borne and wire-borne utilities in a road reserve, including "fibre optic cable", relocated "at no cost to the Authority" on written request, and removed within sixty days of notice |
| Mining Act 2016 | s.153(7) | "A holder of a mineral right shall not commence mining of minerals unless the lawful occupier, owner or user of land is compensated" |
| | s.153(8)–(9) | Those who prefer to be compensated by resettlement are settled on suitable alternative land at the licence holder's cost |
| Community Land Act 2016 | s.36(1) and (3) | Investment agreements on community land follow "a free, open consultative process", cover impact assessment, consultation, ongoing monitoring and evaluation of the investment's impact, compensation and royalties, rehabilitation and mitigation, and need approval by two-thirds of adult members at an assembly with a two-thirds quorum |

**Power-line practice.** The Energy Act 2019 sets no wayleave width for power lines; widths are set per project, in the resettlement action plans. KETRACO's published plans use 40 m for a 220 kV line and 30 m for a 132 kV line, and its land FAQ explains that the corridor is compensated for its use rather than bought, with structures, crops and trees assessed separately and part of the payment made once relocation from the corridor is confirmed. See [power-line wayleaves](/ke/power-line-wayleaves).

**Forests.** A 2026 amendment to the forest law allows the Kenya Forest Service to approve easements and wayleaves in forests; it has been challenged in the Environment and Land Court ([Capital FM, 2 July 2026](https://capitalfm.africa/green-belt-movement-seeks-to-nullify-new-forest-act/)).

::::section{id="offences" tone="alt" eyebrow="Offences" title="What the petroleum and energy laws make an offence"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
**Petroleum Act 2019, s.99.**

- Trespassing or encroaching "on to any petroleum pipeline wayleaves or installations" is an offence carrying a minimum fine or a minimum term of imprisonment (s.99(1)(h)); an attempt carries lower minimums (s.99(2)).
- Illegally acquiring an interest in public land set aside for petroleum infrastructure (s.99(1)(i)), and vandalising, interfering with or illegally interconnecting with a pipeline (s.99(1)(d)), carry a higher minimum fine or at least five years' imprisonment.
::::
::::col
**Energy Act 2019, ss.168–169.**

- Vandalising or damaging a licensee's works carries a minimum fine or at least five years' imprisonment (s.168(4)).
- Wilfully encroaching on, illegally acquiring or dealing in "public land set aside for energy infrastructure projects", and vandalism, theft or damage, are "deemed to be an economic crime", with a minimum fine or ten years' imprisonment; vessels used are forfeited, and civil recovery is possible (s.169).

The amounts are in the Acts; see the Sources below.
