::::section{id="before-mining" eyebrow="Before mining begins" title="Compensation first, and a dated record of what stood there"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
The [Mining Act 2016](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2016/12/eng@2025-06-20) says: "A holder of a mineral right shall not commence mining of minerals unless the lawful occupier, owner or user of land is compensated" (section 153(7)). A licence holder provides a compensation guarantee bond (s.153(2)), and those who "prefer to be compensated by way of resettlement" are settled on suitable alternative land at the licence holder's cost (s.153(8)–(9)).

Each of those steps turns on what stood on the land and when. We record the structures in the licence area and a band around it on dated imagery, from the archive where it exists and on a new capture where it does not, so the census and valuation teams know where to go and the project file shows what was there before mining. Later scans show what has appeared since.

On community land, investment agreements follow "a free, open consultative process" and cover impact assessment, compensation and royalties, rehabilitation and the ongoing monitoring and evaluation of the investment's impact on the community (Community Land Act 2016, s.36(1)). A neutral, dated land-cover and structure baseline gives that monitoring a starting point both sides can see.

::::section{id="licence-areas" tone="alt" eyebrow="Licence areas" title="Your polygon, the ground inside it and the ring around it"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
The Mining Act requires an "up to date computerized mining cadastre and registry system", with a public register of mineral rights (s.191), online applications and a copy kept by the National Land Commission (s.192). The [Mining Cadastre Portal](https://portal.miningcadastre.go.ke/) is where new applicants register.

Against the polygon from your licence or from the register, we map:

- structures inside the licence area and in a ring around it;
- new excavations, pits, spoil heaps and water-filled workings;
- new tracks and access roads into the area;
- cleared ground and vegetation change between dates.

Each feature is listed with its location, its distance to the licence boundary and the dates of the images on either side of the change, and a reviewer confirms it before it reaches you.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="note" title="New mineral rights"}
In March 2026 the government invited expressions of interest for mineral rights in Tharaka Nithi, Embu, Tana River, Kwale and Samburu counties, covering copper, coltan, manganese, niobium, rare earths and chromite ([KBC, 31 March 2026](https://www.kbc.co.ke/government-opens-bid-for-mineral-rights-in-five-counties/)). A new licence area needs a dated baseline before work starts. Tana River County is mapped from satellite imagery only.

::::section{id="artisanal" eyebrow="Artisanal mining" title="Pits, spoil and cleared ground, mapped as land change"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
Artisanal mining in Kenya needs an artisanal mining permit. In July 2026 the government set up a team to reconfirm and verify "demarcated artisanal mining areas" and validate cooperatives ahead of permits at one northern goldfield ([Capital FM, 22 July 2026](https://capitalfm.africa/govt-gives-team-seven-days-to-chart-reopening-of-marsabits-hillo-gold-mines/)). Knowing where the ground has been worked, and where it has not, is part of that work.

We map artisanal workings as land change: pits, disturbed ground, spoil heaps, water-filled excavations, cleared ground and temporary structures, inside and around a licence area or a demarcated area, with new sites flagged between dated images. We map land, never people, and we do not decide whether a site is permitted.

Several of the recent gold rushes and site closures were in West Pokot, where government travel advice warns against travel, as it does for the Turkana and Marsabit border areas. There the work is done from satellite imagery only.
::::
::::col
:::callout{tone="scope" title="What these maps are not"}
- Not a record of who is working a site
- Not a decision on whether a site is permitted
- Not a safety assessment of a pit or a slope
- Not real-time: each flag compares two dated images
:::

::::section{id="closure" tone="alt" eyebrow="Closure and rehabilitation" title="Revegetation tracked between dates"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
After mining, rehabilitation is part of the project's environmental commitments and, on community land, of the investment agreement (Community Land Act s.36(1)). We track the revegetation of rehabilitated land, closed pits and restored access roads between dated images, and map where bare ground remains, so the closure team and the community can see progress on the same map.

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Drone detail at the mine" title="When the site needs more than satellite imagery"}
Stockpile volumes, pit progress and earthworks need drone orthophotos and elevation models. In Kenya those flights are carried out by a company registered in Kenya with a KCAA RPAS Operator Certificate, with the licence holder's and the landowner's permission, subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires. There is no drone-specific rule for mines, and the Official Secrets Act's "prohibited places" include some government sites, so check a site's status before a flight is planned. See [drone law in Kenya](/ke/drone-regulations#restricted).
