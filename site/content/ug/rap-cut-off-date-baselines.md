::::section{id="law" eyebrow="What the law asks for" title="Compensation before possession, and the occupants the law protects" lead="A summary of the public rules as of 27 September 2026, for orientation. It is not legal advice; the sources are linked."}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
### The Constitution and the Supreme Court {#article-26}

Compulsory acquisition needs a public use and "prompt payment of fair and adequate compensation, prior to the taking of possession" ([Constitution](https://ulii.org/akn/ug/act/statute/1995/constitution/eng@2023-12-31), art. 26(2)(b)(i)). In [*UNRA v Irumba & Another*](https://ulii.org/akn/ug/judgment/ugsc/2015/131/eng@2015-10-29) [2015] UGSC 131, the Supreme Court agreed that "Section 7 of the Land Acquisition Act is inconsistent with Article 26(2)"; the Constitutional Court had [nullified s.7(1)](https://ulii.org/akn/ug/judgment/ugcc/2013/17/eng@2013-11-08) ([2013] UGCC 17) to the extent that it allowed possession before payment.

### The Land Acquisition Act {#land-acquisition-act}

Compulsory acquisitions are declared by statutory instrument under the [Land Acquisition Act 1965](https://ulii.org/akn/ug/act/1965/14/eng@2023-12-31) (Cap. 235). UETCL's resettlement framework notes that the Act "does not define specific timelines for payment of compensation, nor does it provide for adjustments to compensation amounts in cases where payment is delayed". No amendment bill is on Parliament's tracker as of 27 September 2026.
::::
::::col
### Land tenure and occupants {#tenure}

"Land in Uganda belongs to the citizens of Uganda", held under customary, freehold, **mailo** or leasehold tenure (art. 237). The [Land Act](https://ulii.org/akn/ug/act/1998/16/eng@2023-12-31) (Cap. 236) protects lawful and bona fide occupants, including **bibanja** holders on mailo land, and UETCL's resettlement framework treats them as eligible for compensation.

### Compensation and valuation {#valuation}

As UETCL's framework summarises the Land Act as enacted, compensation is at depreciated replacement cost in rural areas or market value in urban areas, plus a disturbance allowance of 15 %, or 30 % where less than six months' notice to give vacant possession is given. District compensation rates for crops and trees follow current local market value (Land Regulations 2004, reg 24(1)). The Valuation Bill 2024 was assented to on 19 February 2026; it creates the office of the Chief Government Valuer and a professional institute for valuers.
::::
:::::

:::callout{tone="legal" title="Lender standards"}
Projects financed by the World Bank apply ESS5, which UETCL's framework describes as compensation "prior to displacement, at full replacement cost", with amounts "adjusted to reflect current values" where payment is delayed. Other lenders apply [IFC Performance Standard 5](https://www.ifc.org/content/dam/ifc/doc/2010/2012-ifc-performance-standard-5-en.pdf), which ties eligibility to a census and to a cut-off date that is clearly established and made public. Under both, the dates matter, and a dated structure register supports the record of them.
:::

::::section{id="dated-imagery" tone="alt" eyebrow="The imagery" title="Why the date on the imagery matters"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
A cut-off-date record is only as good as the date on its imagery. We build it on imagery with a known capture date: a very-high-resolution satellite scene with its acquisition date, georeferenced imagery you already hold, or a drone survey flown with the approvals each job requires. Map-service basemaps, which carry no capture date, are never used for it.

Archive coverage and cloud vary by place and season, so the proposal says what exists for your footprint and dates before you commit. A new satellite capture can be requested when the archive is too old; its date depends on satellite availability and weather, and is never guaranteed.

In Uganda a drone baseline also needs UCAA approval, Chief of Defence Forces security clearance and a district sign-off for each operation, and regulation 79 of the drone regulations requires the owner's consent before private property is imaged in a way that could amount to keeping it under observation. See [drone law in Uganda](/ug/drone-regulations).
::::
::::col

::::section{id="data" eyebrow="Personal data" title="Structures on our side, names on yours"}
:::::columns{split="1-1"}
::::col
The [Data Protection and Privacy Act 2019](https://ulii.org/akn/ug/act/2019/9/eng@2023-12-31) governs personal data, and census records are full of it. We keep them apart: our registers list structures with IDs and coordinates, and names, ID numbers and household data stay with your census team. Where you link the two, you do it on your side, through the structure IDs.
::::
::::col
People and vehicles in any drone imagery are blurred, deliverables go only to the recipients you name in writing, and nothing about the project area is published. Processing or storing personal data outside Uganda needs adequate protection in the destination or the data subject's consent, which is one more reason to keep personal data out of the imagery work.

### What it supports

:::checklist

### What it does not do

:::checklist{tone="no"}
- Set the cut-off date or decide who is eligible
- Carry out the census, the valuation or the consultations
- Record tenure: kibanja, mailo, customary or leasehold rights come from the land records and the census
- Count crops or trees, identify households or people, or see roofs hidden under dense tree cover
