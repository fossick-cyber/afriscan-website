faq:
  - q: Which widths do you measure in Uganda?
    a: Yours. For pipelines we report the bands you set from the centreline or from the edge of the right of way, such as the 6 m and 30 m distances in the 2016 Midstream Regulations. For power lines we use the wayleave in your project's resettlement framework, such as the 10 m right of way and 25 m of wayleave either side on a new 400 kV line. For roads we use the reserve width and building line your road authority applies. Up to six widths can be reported in one survey.
  - q: How do we know how old the imagery is?
    a: Every report names its imagery and, where the source gives it, the capture date of each scene, so each feature in the register carries a date. Where the archive is too old for the record you need, very-high-resolution scenes can be sourced or a new capture requested; its date depends on satellite availability and weather.
  - q: Do we need drones?
    a: Usually not. Satellite mapping covers the whole corridor and needs no drone approvals. A drone survey in Uganda needs UCAA approval, Chief of Defence Forces security clearance and a district sign-off for each job, so we keep drone work for the stretches that need centimetre detail, subject to the approvals and security clearances each job requires.
  - q: Will you tell us which structures are unauthorised?
    a: No. We map what stands where, and when it first appeared on dated imagery. Whether a structure is authorised, compensated or eligible is for you, your land team and the authorities to decide.
  - q: Do you map people or households?
    a: No. We map structures, cleared ground, excavations and tracks, never people. People and vehicles in drone imagery are blurred, and names and ID numbers stay with your census and land teams.
  - q: Do you work in the border districts and Karamoja?
    a: We do not offer field or drone work in districts along the DRC and South Sudan borders, or in Karamoja and its neighbouring districts, and every field visit elsewhere follows current travel advice.

::::section{id="law" class="compare" eyebrow="The law already sets the lines" title="The bands your register is measured against" lead="Uganda's rules draw clear lines around linear assets. What asset owners need is a dated, measured record of what stands inside each band, and since when. That is what we provide."}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="Pipelines: 6 m, 30 m and the land next to the line" icon="pipeline" eyebrow="Midstream Regulations 2016" key="oil-gas#law"}
No one may carry out "any activity in an area adjacent to a pipeline system" without PAU's written consent (reg 68). Anyone planning work within 30 m of a pipeline right of way must first find out where the pipe is (reg 92), and machines may dig within 6 m only under the licensee's supervision (reg 97). Licensees survey their routes every six months (reg 87(7)).
:::
:::card{title="Power lines: a corridor where nothing is built" icon="power" eyebrow="Electricity Act · UETCL" key="power-utilities#law"}
A licensee may place lines over any land, paying "prompt, fair, and adequate compensation" for the right of use (Electricity Act s.67). UETCL's published resettlement framework for a new 400 kV interconnector sets a 60 m corridor with no structures, and vegetation and crops no higher than 2 m. The width is set project by project.
:::
:::card{title="Roads: no compensation for unauthorised structures" icon="route" eyebrow="Roads Act 2019" key="rail-roads#law"}
"A person who constructs any structure in or on a road reserve … without authorisation … is not entitled to any compensation" (s.16(8)). The Act also provides for building lines (s.17), and the 2026 road regulations set maximum reserve widths by road class. So the date a structure first appeared matters.
:::
:::card{title="Compensation before possession" icon="calendar" eyebrow="Constitution, art. 26" key="resettlement-cut-off-baselines#law"}
Compulsory acquisition needs "prompt payment of fair and adequate compensation, prior to the taking of possession" (art. 26(2)(b)(i)). A dated record of what stood where supports the cut-off date, the valuation and every later grievance check.
:::
:::

::::section{id="problem" tone="alt" eyebrow="Why it matters" title="What changes between one survey and the next" lead="Pipelines are entering decades of operation, new transmission lines are being prepared, and land for a new railway is being acquired. The land beside them keeps changing."}
:::cards{cols="2"}
:::card{title="“The line is going into operation.”" icon="pipeline"}
A buried pipeline is a right of way to keep clear for decades. Reinstated ground is easy to farm and build on again, and the six-monthly route survey needs a record of what has changed.
:::
:::card{title="“The wayleave was paid for. Now there is a house in it.”" icon="houses"}
A structure inside a registered wayleave is easier to discuss while it is new. A dated register shows whether it stood there before the cut-off date or appeared after it.
:::
:::card{title="“When did that building go up?”" icon="calendar"}
On road reserves the date matters as evidence, because the Roads Act denies compensation for structures built in a reserve without authorisation. Archive imagery shows roughly when a structure first appeared, and the register records it against the reserve width.
:::
:::card{title="“We cannot send a team along the whole route.”" icon="route"}
Long corridors cross farmland, wetlands and growing towns. Satellite mapping covers all of it with no one on site, so field teams go first to the stretches that changed.
:::
:::

UETCL's own [Grid Development Plan 2025–2040](https://uetcl.go.ug/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/GDP25-40.pdf) records that "acquisition of way leaves for construction of transmission lines continues to be a challenge". A dated register does not settle a wayleave. It gives everyone, the utility, the land team and the occupants, the same record of what stood where and when.

::::section{id="standard" tone="dark" eyebrow="Our standard" title="Land and assets, never people"}
AfriScan maps what is on the land: structures, cleared ground, excavations, tracks and vegetation. We do not identify, count or follow people, and we do not decide who is entitled to what. Satellites cannot see theft, vandalism or tapping; they show where the ground has changed, and a person decides what that change means.

::::section{id="drones" eyebrow="Satellite first, drones where they add value" title="Two routes to the picture, and the rules for each"}
:::::columns{split="2-1"}
::::col
**Satellite and your own imagery.** A survey starts from your route file and dated satellite imagery, open building datasets or the orthophotos your own teams already hold. Satellite mapping needs no drone approvals and no one near lines, roads or homes, however long the corridor.

**Drone detail.** Where you need centimetre detail, a drone survey in Uganda needs UCAA approval, a security clearance from the Chief of Defence Forces and district sign-off for each job. We are working towards our own Ugandan drone approvals. Until then, any drone survey we arrange is flown by a Ugandan operator under its own approvals, and we tell you which approvals the job relies on.
